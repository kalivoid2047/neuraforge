<h2 id="math">Two views of the same object</h2>

A vector has (at least) two equally valid ways to think about it, and fluently switching
between them is the actual skill this lesson builds:

<Callout kind="tip" title="Geometric view vs. list-of-numbers view">
  **Geometric**: an arrow with a length and a direction, anchored at the origin.
  **Algebraic**: an ordered list of numbers, `[x, y]` or `[x, y, z]` or, for AI purposes, often
  hundreds or thousands of numbers long. A 2D arrow pointing to `(3, 4)` and the list `[3, 4]`
  are *the same object*, described two ways.
</Callout>

In AI, you'll almost never draw an arrow — a "word embedding" vector might have 768 numbers,
impossible to visualize geometrically in any literal sense. But the geometric intuition (length,
direction, angle between two vectors) still transfers and still guides intuition, even at 768
dimensions where you can no longer draw the picture.

<Callout kind="history" title="Why AI represents everything as vectors">
  A word, an image, a user's preferences — none of these are naturally numbers. Representing
  them as vectors (a process called "embedding," which Month 6 covers in depth) lets every tool
  built for numerical vectors — distance, similarity, the entire apparatus of linear algebra —
  apply directly. This single choice (represent things as vectors) is arguably the foundational
  design decision underlying essentially all of modern AI.
</Callout>

## Summary

A vector is simultaneously a geometric arrow (length and direction) and an ordered list of
numbers — the same object, two views, and fluent movement between them is what this lesson
builds. NumPy represents vectors as 1D arrays of any length; AI systems use vectors of hundreds
or thousands of dimensions to represent words, images, and other non-numeric concepts
numerically, which is the foundational move that makes the rest of linear algebra applicable to
AI at all.
