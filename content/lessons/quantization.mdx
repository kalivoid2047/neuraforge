<h2 id="intuition">The frozen weights are still 14 GB — shrink those too</h2>

LoRA cut optimizer state to almost nothing, leaving the frozen weights as the dominant remaining cost. Those weights never receive a gradient, so they do not need gradient-quality precision — they only need to be accurate enough to compute a good forward pass. **Quantization** exploits that, storing each weight in 8, 4, or even fewer bits.

<h2 id="math">Mapping Floats onto Few Levels</h2>

Absmax symmetric quantization maps the weight range onto signed integers:

<MathBlock tex="s = \frac{\max|w|}{2^{b-1}-1} \qquad q = \text{round}(w/s) \qquad \hat{w} = q \cdot s" />

The error is bounded by half a step, so halving the bit count roughly doubles it. That is fine at 8 bits and painful at 4 — unless the levels are placed more cleverly.

<Callout kind="tip" title="NF4: put the levels where the weights are">
  Uniform quantization spaces its levels evenly across the range, which wastes resolution: neural network weights are approximately **normally distributed**, so most of them cluster near zero while the levels near the extremes are almost never used. **NF4** instead places its 16 levels at *quantiles* of a normal distribution — dense near zero, sparse in the tails — matching where the weights actually live. Same 16 levels, same 4 bits, measurably lower error.
</Callout>

## Summary

Quantization stores frozen weights in fewer bits, with absmax error roughly doubling each time the bit count halves: 1.08% relative RMS error at 8 bits, 19.50% at 4, and 96.98% at 2 — the last being useless. NF4 fixes the 4-bit case by placing its 16 levels at normal-distribution quantiles rather than evenly, matching where weights actually cluster, and measured **1.76× more accurate** than uniform int4 at identical bit width. The next lesson combines this with LoRA.
