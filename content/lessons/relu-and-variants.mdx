<h2 id="intuition">The activation function that fixed (most of) saturation</h2>

ReLU — Rectified Linear Unit — is almost absurdly simple: output the input unchanged if
positive, output zero otherwise. That simplicity is exactly the point: for any positive input,
its gradient is a constant 1, no matter how large the input gets — no saturation, unlike
sigmoid/tanh. This single property was a major factor in enabling the much deeper networks that
became practical starting in the early 2010s.

<h2 id="math">ReLU, and its dying-neuron problem</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\mathrm{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z), \qquad \mathrm{ReLU}'(z) = \begin{cases}1 & z > 0\\ 0 & z < 0\end{cases}" />

<Callout kind="warning" title="The 'dying ReLU' problem">
  ReLU's gradient is exactly zero for any negative input — meaning if a neuron's weights end up
  such that it always outputs a negative value before activation, its gradient is permanently
  zero and it stops learning entirely ("dies"), for every future training step. This is a real,
  observed failure mode in practice, not just a theoretical concern.
</Callout>

**Leaky ReLU** fixes this with a small nonzero slope for negative inputs, instead of a hard
zero:

<MathBlock tex="\mathrm{LeakyReLU}(z) = \begin{cases}z & z > 0\\ \alpha z & z \le 0\end{cases} \qquad \text{(typically } \alpha = 0.01\text{)}" />

A small nonzero gradient even in the "negative" regime means a neuron can still receive some
learning signal and potentially recover, rather than dying permanently.

<h2 id="python">Comparing ReLU, Leaky ReLU, and GELU</h2>

<Callout kind="tip" title="Which one should you actually use?">
  Plain ReLU remains a strong, fast, simple default for many architectures. Leaky ReLU is a
  cheap insurance policy against dying neurons. GELU (and its relatives) are the standard choice
  in modern Transformer architectures — smoother, slightly better empirical performance in
  practice, at a small extra computational cost. The next lesson covers softmax, which serves an
  entirely different role: not a hidden-layer activation, but the standard choice for
  multi-class output layers.
</Callout>

## Summary

ReLU's constant gradient of 1 for positive inputs avoids the saturation that plagues
sigmoid/tanh, but its exactly-zero gradient for negative inputs can permanently "kill" a neuron
during training. Leaky ReLU patches this with a small nonzero negative-side slope; smoother
variants like GELU are standard in modern Transformer architectures. The right choice depends on
the architecture — this lesson gives you the vocabulary and trade-offs to make that call.
