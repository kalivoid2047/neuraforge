<h2 id="intuition">Two failure modes, one underlying trade-off</h2>

Month 4's overfitting lesson showed a high-degree polynomial memorizing noise. That's one end of
a spectrum called **bias-variance trade-off**: a model with too little capacity has **high
bias** (it can't represent the true pattern at all, underfitting both train and validation
data); a model with too much capacity has **high variance** (it fits training-set-specific noise
that doesn't generalize, the classic overfitting signature). Between these extremes sits a
capacity that fits the real pattern without memorizing noise — found using cross-validation
(last lesson), not by staring at training accuracy alone.

<h2 id="math">Bias, variance, and capacity</h2>

As model capacity (e.g., a neural network's hidden layer size) increases:

- **Training error** decreases roughly monotonically — more capacity means fitting the training
  data more and more exactly.
- **Cross-validated error** decreases at first (reducing bias, learning the real pattern), then
  *increases* again (rising variance, memorizing noise) — an approximately **U-shaped** curve.

The capacity at the bottom of that U is the sweet spot — genuinely the best-performing choice on
new data, not the choice with the lowest training error (which is always the *largest* capacity
tried, by construction, and is exactly the wrong signal to optimize).

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why this generalizes last month's overfitting lesson">
  Month 4 showed one *specific instance* of high variance (a degree-11 polynomial). Bias-
  variance trade-off is the *general principle* behind that specific example — applicable to
  polynomial degree, neural network width or depth, or, as verified below, the hidden layer size
  of the text classifier built last lesson.
</Callout>

## Summary

Bias-variance trade-off generalizes Month 4's overfitting lesson into a full spectrum: too
little model capacity underfits (high bias, poor performance everywhere), too much overfits
(high variance, memorizing training-set noise), and cross-validation traces out the resulting
U-shaped curve needed to find the sweet spot in between — verified above across several hidden
layer sizes for the text classifier. Training accuracy alone always favors the largest capacity
tried, which is precisely the wrong signal for choosing a model to deploy. The final lesson this
month assembles everything — probability, information theory, classical NLP, evaluation, and
this bias-variance methodology — into the Text Classifier capstone.
