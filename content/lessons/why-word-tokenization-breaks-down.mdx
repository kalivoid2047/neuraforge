<h2 id="intuition">Month 6: from bag-of-words counting to real language model tokenizers</h2>

Month 5's `tokenize` function split text on whitespace and punctuation, then looked words up in
a vocabulary built from the training corpus. That works for a small classroom example, but it
has a fatal flaw at real scale: **any word never seen during vocabulary-building has no
representation at all.** A model trained on English news text has literally no way to handle a
typo, a brand-new word, a name, or a different language — it either crashes or silently drops
the word, discarding whatever information it carried. This month builds the tokenization,
embedding, and language-modeling machinery that makes today's LLMs handle *any* input text, not
just text resembling their training vocabulary.

<h2 id="math">The OOV problem, precisely</h2>

For a word-level vocabulary `V` built from a training corpus, any word `w \notin V` encountered
at inference time is **out-of-vocabulary (OOV)** — the model has no embedding, no learned
representation, nothing to do with it except a generic `<UNK>` placeholder that throws away all
information the specific word carried.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why you can't just make the vocabulary bigger">
  English alone has hundreds of thousands of word forms (plurals, verb tenses, and so on), names
  are essentially infinite, and typos/misspellings multiply that further. Covering even 99% of
  real-world text with whole words requires a vocabulary so large that the embedding table
  itself (one vector per vocabulary entry) becomes a huge fraction of the entire model's
  parameters — and the last 1% (rare words, new words, other languages) is *still* uncovered, no
  matter how large the vocabulary grows.
</Callout>

## Summary

Word-level tokenization fails because the space of possible words is effectively unbounded —
any finite vocabulary leaves some words permanently out-of-vocabulary, replaced by a generic
`<UNK>` token that discards all their information — verified directly above with "cats" and
"kitten" both falling outside a small vocabulary built from ordinary English sentences. Making
the vocabulary larger only partially helps, at the cost of a larger embedding table, and never
fully closes the gap. The next lesson builds Byte-Pair Encoding, the subword tokenization
algorithm that solves this by working below the whole-word level.
