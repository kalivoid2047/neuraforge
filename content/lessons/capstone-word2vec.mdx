<h2 id="intuition">The month's arc, in one pipeline</h2>

Trace the path from the first lesson to here: the OOV problem → BPE → WordPiece → SentencePiece
and Viterbi segmentation → tokenizer compression trade-offs → one-hot's missing geometry → the
distributional hypothesis → skip-gram and CBOW → negative sampling → GloVe → language models →
n-gram smoothing → perplexity → RNNs → vanishing gradients → skip-gram gradients → embedding
evaluation → PCA. This capstone uses the core of it: a real corpus, tokenized, trained with
skip-gram plus negative sampling, and then *evaluated properly* rather than declared successful.

<h2 id="math">The full pipeline</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\text{corpus} \xrightarrow{\text{tokenize}} \text{tokens} \xrightarrow{\text{window}} (c,o)\ \text{pairs} \xrightarrow{\text{SGNS}} W_{in} \xrightarrow{\text{evaluate}} \text{nearest neighbours, similarity, PCA}" />

The deliverable is `W_in` — one dense vector per vocabulary word — plus an honest evaluation of
whether its geometry actually captures the corpus's semantic structure.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Reporting the PCA number is the honest part">
  It would be easy to stop at EVAL 1, show a few plausible nearest neighbours, and declare
  success — and EVAL 1 above genuinely does look good (`plane → pilot, flight`). But EVAL 2
  shows the within-vs-cross-domain separation is only `+0.25`, and EVAL 3 shows the top two
  principal components capture just **23.7%** of the variance, meaning a 2D scatter plot of
  these embeddings would hide roughly three-quarters of their actual structure. Both are honest,
  unremarkable numbers for a 15-sentence corpus — and reporting them is exactly the point of
  building an evaluation *suite* instead of cherry-picking one favourable example.
</Callout>

## Month 6 summary

This month built the representation layer that sits between raw text and a language model.
Week one solved the out-of-vocabulary problem with subword tokenization — BPE's frequency-based
merging, WordPiece's likelihood criterion, SentencePiece's language-agnostic Viterbi
segmentation — and measured the real compression trade-offs that follow from a tokenizer's
training distribution. Week two gave tokens a geometry: one-hot's total absence of similarity
structure, the distributional hypothesis that makes embeddings learnable from unlabeled text,
Word2Vec's skip-gram/CBOW objectives, negative sampling, and GloVe's co-occurrence-ratio
framing. Week three built language modeling itself: the chain rule factorization, n-gram
smoothing, perplexity, RNNs, and the vanishing gradient problem that caps what recurrence can
learn. Week four trained and honestly evaluated real embeddings, closing with this capstone.

**Month 7 begins exactly where week three's final lesson left off**: attention was invented
specifically to solve the long-range dependency problem that vanishing gradients make
unreachable for RNNs.
