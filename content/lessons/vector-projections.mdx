<h2 id="intuition">The shadow a vector casts on another direction</h2>

Imagine shining a light straight down onto vector `a`, casting a shadow onto the line defined by
vector `b`. That shadow — the closest point on `b`'s line to the tip of `a` — is the
**projection** of `a` onto `b`. It answers a concrete question: "how much of `a` points in the
`b` direction?"

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why this matters, ahead">
  Projection is the geometric operation underneath least-squares regression (fitting the "best"
  line through data), and it's the same core idea (measuring how much one vector aligns with
  another) that reappears as the dot product inside attention, back in Month 7 — you're building
  the same tool from a different angle.
</Callout>

<h2 id="math">The projection formula</h2>

The projection of `a` onto `b`:

<MathBlock tex="\mathrm{proj}_{\vec{b}}(\vec{a}) = \frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{\vec{b}\cdot\vec{b}}\,\vec{b}" />

Read the scalar part, `(a·b)/(b·b)`, as "how far along `b`'s direction the shadow falls" — then
that scalar multiplies `b` itself to produce an actual vector lying exactly on `b`'s line. Every
vector splits cleanly into a piece parallel to `b` (the projection) and a piece perpendicular to
`b` (the remainder):

<MathBlock tex="\vec{a} = \underbrace{\mathrm{proj}_{\vec{b}}(\vec{a})}_{\text{parallel to } \vec{b}} + \underbrace{\left(\vec{a} - \mathrm{proj}_{\vec{b}}(\vec{a})\right)}_{\text{perpendicular to } \vec{b}}" />

<Callout kind="warning" title="Pitfall: forgetting to divide by b·b">
  A common mistake is projecting using `(a·b) * b` without dividing by `b·b` first — this only
  gives the correct projection when `b` happens to already be a unit vector (length 1). For any
  other length, the division is what correctly cancels out `b`'s own magnitude from the scalar
  coefficient. If you know `b` is already normalized, the formula simplifies to `(a·b) * b` —
  but that's a special case, not the general rule.
</Callout>

## Summary

The projection of `a` onto `b` is `((a·b)/(b·b)) · b` — the "shadow" `a` casts onto `b`'s
direction, computed by scaling `b` by exactly how much `a` aligns with it. Every vector
decomposes cleanly into a component parallel to a reference direction (the projection) and one
perpendicular to it (verified by a zero dot product with `b`). This is the geometric backbone of
least-squares fitting and reappears throughout this course wherever "how aligned are these two
things" needs a precise answer.
