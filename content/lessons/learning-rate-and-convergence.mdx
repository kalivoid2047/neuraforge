<h2 id="intuition">The single most important hyperparameter</h2>

The learning rate `η` from the update rule (two lessons ago) is arguably the single most
consequential number you choose when training a model. Too small, and training crawls,
technically correct but wasting enormous amounts of compute. Too large, and training doesn't
converge at all — it can oscillate indefinitely or blow up to infinity. This lesson makes both
failure modes concrete, numerically, on the same simple function from the gradient descent
lesson.

<h2 id="math">Three regimes</h2>

For `f(w) = (w-3)² + 1`, whose derivative is `f'(w) = 2(w-3)`, the update rule becomes:

<MathBlock tex="w_{new} = w - \eta \cdot 2(w - 3) = (1 - 2\eta)\,w + 6\eta" />

The factor `(1 - 2η)` determines everything: if `|1 - 2η| < 1`, each step shrinks the distance
to the minimum, and the process converges. If `|1 - 2η| > 1`, each step *grows* the distance —
divergence. For this specific quadratic, that boundary is exactly `η = 1.0`: below it,
convergence (slower as `η` shrinks further from the ideal); above it, divergence.

<h2 id="python">Three learning rates, same starting point, three very different outcomes</h2>

<CodeCell
  code={"def f(w):\n    return (w - 3) ** 2 + 1\ndef f_prime(w):\n    return 2 * (w - 3)\n\ndef run_gradient_descent(learning_rate, n_steps=10, w_start=0.0):\n    w = w_start\n    trajectory = [w]\n    for _ in range(n_steps):\n        w = w - learning_rate * f_prime(w)\n        trajectory.append(w)\n    return trajectory\n\nfor lr, label in [(0.01, \"too small\"), (0.4, \"well-chosen\"), (1.2, \"too large\")]:\n    traj = run_gradient_descent(lr)\n    print(f\"learning_rate={lr} ({label}):\")\n    print(\"  w values:\", [round(w, 3) for w in traj])\n    print(\"  final f(w):\", round(f(traj[-1]), 4), \" (true minimum f=1.0)\")\n    print()"}

<Callout kind="tip" title="Divergence isn't subtle -- it's exponential">
  Watch the `too large` trajectory: once `|1-2η|>1`, each step doesn't just fail to improve, it
  makes the distance to the minimum *larger* by a constant multiplicative factor every step —
  the same kind of exponential blowup as compound interest running in the wrong direction. This
  is why a bad learning rate is usually obvious quickly (loss shooting toward infinity or `NaN`
  within a few steps) rather than a slow, hard-to-notice degradation.
</Callout>

## Summary

The learning rate controls a fundamental trade-off: too small wastes compute on unnecessarily
slow convergence, too large causes oscillation or outright divergence, with loss growing
exponentially rather than shrinking. For the simple quadratic examined here, convergence versus
divergence has a sharp, computable boundary — real loss surfaces are more complex, but the same
qualitative failure modes appear, which is why choosing (or scheduling) the learning rate is one
of the most important practical decisions in training any model. The final lesson this week
assembles a complete training loop and puts everything this month built to work.
