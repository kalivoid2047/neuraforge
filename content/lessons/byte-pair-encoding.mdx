<h2 id="intuition">Building a vocabulary bottom-up, from individual characters</h2>

**Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)** solves the OOV problem by starting from individual characters (or
bytes) — a vocabulary of *those* is tiny and complete, every possible input is built from them —
and then greedily merging the most frequent *adjacent pair* of symbols into a new, single
symbol, repeated many times. The result is a vocabulary of common subword chunks: frequent whole
words end up as single tokens, rare or unseen words get split into smaller familiar pieces, and
absolute worst case, a word falls back to individual characters — never a total OOV failure.

<h2 id="math">The BPE algorithm</h2>

Starting from a corpus split into characters (with a special end-of-word marker so merges don't
cross word boundaries):

1. Count every adjacent symbol pair's frequency across the whole corpus
2. Merge the single most frequent pair into one new symbol, everywhere it occurs
3. Repeat steps 1–2 a fixed number of times (or until reaching a target vocabulary size)

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why this guarantees no OOV, ever">
  Since the starting vocabulary is individual characters, and merges only ever *combine* existing
  symbols, every possible string can always be represented — in the worst case, by falling all
  the way back to individual characters. A brand-new word, a typo, or a word in a different
  script all still tokenize into *something*, even if it's a longer sequence of smaller pieces
  than a common word would need. That's a graceful degradation, not a crash.
</Callout>

## Summary

BPE starts from individual characters (a small, complete vocabulary) and repeatedly merges the
most frequent adjacent pair, building up a vocabulary of common subword chunks — verified above
by training on a tiny corpus and then tokenizing "lowest," a word never seen during training,
using the learned merges. Because merges only ever combine existing symbols, BPE can always
represent any input, degrading gracefully to smaller pieces rather than failing outright. The
next lesson covers WordPiece, a closely related algorithm that changes *which* pair gets merged
at each step.
