<h2 id="intuition">Week three: predicting what comes next</h2>

A **language model** assigns a probability to any sequence of tokens — high for fluent,
plausible text and low for gibberish. That single capability is more powerful than it sounds:
because of how sequence probability factors, a model that can assign probabilities to sequences
is *exactly* a model that can predict the next token given everything before it — which is the
entire basis of text generation, and the training objective behind every LLM covered in the
rest of this curriculum.

<h2 id="math">The chain rule of probability</h2>

The probability of a whole sequence factors into a product of next-token conditional
probabilities — a direct application of the conditional probability definition from Month 5,
week one, applied repeatedly:

<MathBlock tex="P(w_1, w_2, \ldots, w_n) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} P(w_i \mid w_1, \ldots, w_{i-1})" />

This factorization is why "assign probability to sequences" and "predict the next token" are the
same problem. Training maximizes this sequence probability — which, taking logs (Month 5, week
two), is exactly maximum likelihood estimation, and equivalently cross-entropy minimization over
next-token predictions.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Generation is just repeated sampling from the next-token distribution">
  Once you can compute `P(next token | everything so far)`, generating text is straightforward:
  sample a token from that distribution, append it to the context, and repeat. Every difference
  between a coherent LLM response and random noise comes down to how well the model estimates
  that one conditional distribution — and how you sample from it (a topic Month 8 covers in
  depth: temperature, top-k, top-p).
</Callout>

## Summary

A language model is a probability distribution over token sequences, and the chain rule of
probability factors any sequence's probability into a product of next-token conditionals —
making "score a sequence" and "predict the next token" the same capability, verified above with
a bigram model that does both from one set of estimated probabilities, plus generates text by
repeatedly sampling from its next-token distribution. The next lesson makes n-gram language
models practical by addressing what happens when an unseen n-gram makes the whole sequence
probability collapse to zero.
