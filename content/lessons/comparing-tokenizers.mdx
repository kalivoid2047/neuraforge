<h2 id="intuition">Closing week one: a tokenizer is only as good as its training distribution</h2>

BPE, WordPiece, and Unigram LM all learn their subword vocabulary *from a training corpus* —
which means the resulting tokenizer is optimized specifically for whatever distribution of text
that corpus represents. Text resembling the training distribution gets efficiently compressed
into a few learned subword chunks; text unlike it gets fragmented into many small pieces,
falling back toward character-level tokenization. This has a genuine, well-documented real-world
consequence: language models whose tokenizers were trained mostly on English text produce
noticeably *more* tokens for other languages representing the same amount of actual content —
directly translating into higher API costs and a smaller effective context window for speakers
of those languages.

<h2 id="math">Compression ratio</h2>

A simple, useful measure of tokenizer efficiency on some text:

<MathBlock tex="\text{compression ratio} = \frac{\text{number of characters}}{\text{number of tokens}}" />

A higher ratio means each token, on average, represents more characters — better compression. A
ratio at or below `1.0` means the tokenizer is essentially falling back to individual
characters (or worse, once a per-word end-marker token is counted) — exactly the degradation
this month's BPE lesson showed happens gracefully (never crashing) but inefficiently for
unfamiliar text.

<Callout kind="tip" title="This is a real, documented issue -- not just a toy example">
  Because most widely-used tokenizers were trained on corpora dominated by English (and a
  handful of other high-resource languages), the same sentence translated into a low-resource
  language routinely takes 2-4x more tokens to represent. Since API pricing and context-window
  limits are both measured in tokens, this means users writing in those languages pay more and
  fit less into the same context window, for text conveying the *same* amount of information —
  a direct, measurable fairness consequence of a training-data choice made long before any
  individual user's query.
</Callout>

## Week one summary

This week built subword tokenization from first principles: BPE's bottom-up frequency-based
merging, WordPiece's likelihood-based merge criterion, SentencePiece's language-agnostic
whitespace handling and Unigram LM probabilistic segmentation via Viterbi, and finally a direct
measurement of how a tokenizer's training distribution determines its real-world compression
efficiency — with genuine fairness consequences for text unlike that distribution. Every one of
these algorithms exists to solve the OOV problem from the first lesson, each with a different
mechanism. Week two turns to what happens *after* tokenization: representing each token as a
dense vector that captures something about its meaning.
