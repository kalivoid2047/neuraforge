<h2 id="intuition">Month three: answering the cliffhanger from Month 2</h2>

Month 2 ended with a genuine, working neural network that could not learn — its weights were
fixed at random initialization forever, because nothing told them how to change. This month
closes that gap completely, starting from the most basic building block: the **derivative**,
which measures how a function's output changes as its input changes, infinitesimally. By the
end of this month, you'll have built an autograd engine and used it to actually train Month 2's
network.

<Callout kind="tip" title="The one-sentence intuition">
  A derivative answers: "if I nudge the input by a tiny amount, how much (and in which
  direction) does the output move?" That's it — everything else this month builds on that single
  question, asked of increasingly complex functions.
</Callout>

<h2 id="math">The limit definition</h2>

<MathBlock tex="f'(x) = \lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}" />

This is the **difference quotient** — "change in output" over "change in input" — in the limit
as the input change `h` shrinks to zero. Geometrically, it's the slope of the line tangent to
`f`'s graph at the point `x`.

<h2 id="python">Approximating a derivative numerically</h2>

<CodeCell
  code={"def numerical_derivative(f, x, h=1e-7):\n    \"\"\"Approximate f'(x) using a small but nonzero h -- the limit definition, not taken literally to 0.\"\"\"\n    return (f(x + h) - f(x)) / h\n\ndef f(x):\n    return x ** 2\n\n# The exact derivative of x^2 is 2x -- known from algebra, useful as ground truth here\nfor x in [0, 1, 2, 5, -3]:\n    exact = 2 * x\n    approx = numerical_derivative(f, x)\n    print(f\"x={x:>3}: exact f'(x)={exact:>4}, numerical approximation={approx:.4f}\")\n\n# Watch the approximation get WORSE if h is too large or too small\nprint()\nprint(\"effect of h's size, at x=3 (exact answer: 6):\")\nfor h in [1e-1, 1e-4, 1e-7, 1e-12, 1e-16]:\n    approx = numerical_derivative(f, 3, h=h)\n    print(f\"  h={h:.0e}: approximation={approx}\")"}

<Callout kind="warning" title="Why the approximation degrades at very small h">
  With `h` too large, the difference quotient measures the *average* slope over a wide interval,
  not the instantaneous slope — a real approximation error. With `h` too *small* (like `1e-16`),
  a different problem appears: floating-point precision. `f(x+h)` and `f(x)` become nearly
  indistinguishable in floating-point representation, and subtracting two nearly-equal numbers
  amplifies rounding error catastrophically (a phenomenon called "catastrophic cancellation").
  There's a sweet spot, not "smaller is always better" — a preview of exactly why the autograd
  engine this month builds computes derivatives *symbolically*, not by this numerical
  approximation, for anything beyond quick sanity checks.
</Callout>

## Summary

The derivative measures instantaneous rate of change — the limit of the difference quotient
`(f(x+h) - f(x))/h` as `h → 0`. Numerically approximating it with a small nonzero `h` works, but
has a real sweet spot: too large introduces approximation error, too small introduces
floating-point cancellation error. This tension is exactly why the autograd engine this month
builds computes derivatives symbolically/algorithmically rather than via numerical
approximation — though numerical approximation remains a valuable *sanity check* tool, used
later this month to test that engine's correctness.
