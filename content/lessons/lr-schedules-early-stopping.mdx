<h2 id="intuition">Solving Adam's fixed-step problem, and knowing when to stop</h2>

Recall from this month's optimizer lesson: Adam's step size stays roughly constant under a
fixed learning rate, which can leave it hovering near a minimum rather than settling exactly —
unlike plain SGD, whose step naturally shrinks as the gradient shrinks. A **learning rate
schedule** fixes this directly: deliberately shrink `η` over the course of training, so *any*
optimizer's steps get smaller as training progresses, encouraging fine settling late rather than
large jumps forever. **Early stopping** solves a related but separate problem: knowing *when*
to stop training at all, using validation loss (not training loss) as the signal, since
training loss can keep falling long after the model has started overfitting.

<h2 id="math">Two schedules, one stopping rule</h2>

**Step decay** — multiply the learning rate by a fixed factor every few epochs:

<MathBlock tex="\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot \gamma^{\lfloor t / s \rfloor}" />

**Cosine annealing** — smoothly decrease from `η₀` to (near) zero following a cosine curve,
rather than in discrete jumps:

<MathBlock tex="\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \cos\left(\frac{t}{T}\pi\right)\right)" />

**Early stopping**: track validation loss every epoch; if it hasn't improved for `patience`
consecutive epochs, stop training and keep the weights from the best epoch seen (not the last
one) — because after that point, further training is fitting noise, not signal, exactly the
overfitting pattern demonstrated two lessons ago.

```python
# reference only -- torch isn't available in this browser sandbox
import torch.optim as optim

optimizer = optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=0.01)
scheduler = optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=100)
for epoch in range(100):
    train_one_epoch(...)
    scheduler.step()   # updates optimizer's learning rate for the NEXT epoch
```

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why keep the BEST epoch's weights, not the LAST epoch's">
  By the time early stopping actually triggers, several epochs have already passed with rising
  validation loss — the weights at the *stopping* epoch are already somewhat overfit. That's
  exactly why a real early-stopping implementation checkpoints (saves) the model's weights every
  time validation loss improves, and restores that saved checkpoint at the end — using the
  *best* epoch's weights, not whatever the last epoch happened to leave behind.
</Callout>

## Summary

Learning rate schedules (step decay, cosine annealing) deliberately shrink the learning rate
over training, letting any optimizer's steps get finer and settle more precisely as training
progresses — addressing the fixed-step-size limitation of optimizers like Adam. Early stopping
tracks validation loss (not training loss, since that can keep improving even as the model
overfits) and halts training once it stops improving for `patience` epochs, keeping the best
checkpoint rather than the last one. Both are practical techniques the final lesson's capstone
puts to use. The capstone assembles this entire month — tensors, autograd, `nn.Module`,
optimizers, regularization, and convolution — into one complete Image Classifier.
