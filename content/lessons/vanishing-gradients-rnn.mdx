<h2 id="intuition">Closing week three: RNNs can't actually use the long memory they theoretically have</h2>

Last lesson claimed an RNN's hidden state *can in principle* carry information from any earlier
position. In practice, it usually can't — and the reason is a direct consequence of the chain
rule from Month 3. To learn a dependency spanning 50 timesteps, the gradient must propagate
back through 50 recurrence steps, and at each one it gets multiplied by a factor. Multiply 50
numbers slightly less than 1 together and the result is essentially zero: the **vanishing
gradient problem**. The early timesteps receive almost no learning signal, so long-range
dependencies never get learned, no matter how long you train.

<h2 id="math">Backpropagation through time</h2>

The gradient of the loss at timestep `t` with respect to a hidden state `k` steps earlier
involves a product of Jacobians (Month 3, week two), one per intervening step:

<MathBlock tex="\frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-k}} = \prod_{i=t-k+1}^{t} \frac{\partial h_i}{\partial h_{i-1}} = \prod_{i} W_{hh}^\top \text{diag}(\tanh'(\cdot))" />

Two multiplicative factors compound at every step: `W_hh`'s magnitude, and `tanh'` — which is
`1 - tanh²`, at most `1` and typically well below it (recall Month 4's saturation discussion).
Their product raised to the power `k` shrinks **exponentially** with distance.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Exploding gradients: the same mechanism, the opposite direction">
  If `W_hh` has a large enough magnitude, the same repeated multiplication makes gradients
  *explode* toward infinity instead. That failure is far easier to fix — gradient clipping caps
  the norm and training proceeds. Vanishing is the harder problem: there's no "un-clipping" that
  recovers a signal already multiplied down to essentially zero. LSTMs and GRUs were designed
  specifically to mitigate this with gated pathways that let gradients flow more directly across
  timesteps.
</Callout>

## Week three summary

This week built language modeling from the ground up: a language model is a probability
distribution over sequences, factored by the chain rule into next-token predictions; n-gram
models implement this by counting, but need smoothing to avoid a single unseen n-gram zeroing an
entire sequence; perplexity (exponentiated cross-entropy) measures model quality as an effective
branching factor; RNNs escape the n-gram sparsity trap via a carried hidden state and shared
weights; but the vanishing gradient problem means they can't actually learn the long-range
dependencies their architecture theoretically permits — verified above by measuring a gradient
shrink by a factor of over 10¹³ across 50 timesteps (and, with a larger weight scale, explode
by a comparable factor in the other direction). **That specific failure is precisely the problem
attention solves**, which is exactly where Month 7 begins. Week four applies everything from
weeks one and two to build this month's Word2Vec capstone.
