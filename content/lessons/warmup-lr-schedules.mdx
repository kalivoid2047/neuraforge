<h2 id="intuition">Why the learning rate cannot be constant</h2>

Month 4 introduced learning rate schedules and the capstone at the end of this month will show a run that diverges without one. Transformers add a second requirement on top of decay: the learning rate must also **start near zero and ramp up**. Skipping that warmup is one of the most reliable ways to make a large Transformer diverge in its first few hundred steps.

<h2 id="math">Warmup Then Decay</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\eta_t = \eta_{\max}\cdot\frac{t}{t_{\text{warmup}}} \quad (t < t_{\text{warmup}})" />

<MathBlock tex="\eta_t = \eta_{\max}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(1+\cos\left(\pi\frac{t-t_{\text{warmup}}}{T-t_{\text{warmup}}}\right)\right) \quad (t \geq t_{\text{warmup}})" />

The schedule rises linearly to a peak at the end of warmup, then decays smoothly to zero.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why Adam in particular needs warmup">
  Adam divides by `√v̂`, its running estimate of squared-gradient magnitude. At step 1 that estimate is built from a *single* gradient, so it is almost pure noise — and dividing by a noisy, near-zero denominator produces enormous, effectively random parameter updates. Warmup keeps the step size small until `v` has accumulated enough samples to be a meaningful scale. This is why plain SGD often trains fine without warmup while Adam frequently does not.
</Callout>

## Summary

Transformer schedules combine linear warmup with cosine decay: the rate climbs from `6.000e-07` at step 1 to a peak of `3.000e-04` exactly at the end of warmup, then decays to zero. Warmup exists because Adam's second-moment estimate is unreliable when built from a handful of gradients, making early full-size steps effectively random. The next lesson covers how to evaluate the model this schedule trains.
