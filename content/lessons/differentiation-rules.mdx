<h2 id="intuition">Shortcuts that avoid re-deriving from the limit every time</h2>

Computing every derivative from the limit definition would be unbearably slow. In practice,
a handful of rules let you compute derivatives of complicated expressions by breaking them into
pieces and combining the pieces' known derivatives — exactly the strategy an autograd engine
(week three) automates.

<h2 id="math">The rules</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\text{Power rule: } \frac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}" />
<MathBlock tex="\text{Sum rule: } \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)+g(x)] = f'(x)+g'(x)" />
<MathBlock tex="\text{Product rule: } \frac{d}{dx}[f(x)g(x)] = f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)" />
<MathBlock tex="\text{Quotient rule: } \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{f(x)}{g(x)}\right] = \frac{f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{g(x)^2}" />

<Callout kind="tip" title="Where the product rule actually comes from">
  It's not arbitrary: think of `f(x)g(x)` as the area of a rectangle with sides `f(x)` and
  `g(x)`. Nudge `x` by a tiny `h` — both sides change slightly, and the area's change splits into
  two thin strips (one from each side changing) plus a vanishingly small corner piece. Those two
  strips are exactly `f'(x)g(x)` and `f(x)g'(x)`.
</Callout>

## Summary

The power, sum, product, and quotient rules let you compute derivatives of composite expressions
from the known derivatives of their pieces, without re-deriving from the limit definition each
time. Each rule checks out against the numerical approximation from last lesson — a pattern
(symbolic computation, numerically verified) that reappears when the autograd engine's
correctness gets tested later this month.
