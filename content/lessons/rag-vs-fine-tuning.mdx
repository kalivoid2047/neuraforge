<h2 id="intuition">The question is not which is better</h2>

Month 9 adapted a model by changing its weights; Month 10 adapts it by changing what it sees. They are routinely posed as alternatives, which is the wrong framing — they address different failures and combine cleanly.

<h2 id="math">Different Tools, Different Problems</h2>

Where each one wins:

<table>
<thead><tr><th></th><th>fine-tuning</th><th>RAG</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>update cost</td><td>a retraining run</td><td>reindex one document</td></tr>
<tr><td>latency</td><td>none added</td><td>retrieval + longer prompt</td></tr>
<tr><td>freshness</td><td>frozen at training cutoff</td><td>current</td></tr>
<tr><td>attribution</td><td>impossible</td><td>citations</td></tr>
<tr><td>changes behaviour</td><td>yes</td><td>weakly, via the prompt</td></tr>
<tr><td>changes knowledge</td><td>yes, expensively</td><td>yes, cheaply</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

The rule of thumb from lesson 10.1.1, now with a month of evidence behind it: **fine-tune for behaviour, retrieve for knowledge.**

<Callout kind="tip" title="Production systems use both">
  A typical deployment fine-tunes for format, tone, and domain register — the things that should be stable — and retrieves for facts that change. Month 9's capstone showed LoRA adapting behaviour while preserving capability at `1.000` retention; this month shows retrieval supplying current, citable facts. Neither substitutes for the other, and choosing between them is usually a false dichotomy.
</Callout>

## Summary

Fine-tuning changes weights; RAG changes context. Fine-tuning wins on latency and behavioural control; RAG wins on update cost, freshness, and attribution. The rule is fine-tune for behaviour and retrieve for knowledge, and the two compose — Month 9's LoRA preserved capability at `1.000` retention while adapting behaviour, and retrieval supplies current citable facts on top. Treating them as competitors is usually a false dichotomy. The next lesson assembles the full evaluation.
