<h2 id="intuition">A one-line change that decides whether a deep stack trains</h2>

Month 7 built blocks with LayerNorm *after* the residual addition — **post-LN**, exactly as the
original Transformer paper specified. It works fine at 6 layers. At 40 or 96 layers it becomes
notoriously difficult to train, needing a carefully tuned learning-rate warmup to converge at
all. Moving the LayerNorm *inside* the residual branch — **pre-LN** — largely fixes this, and is
what essentially every modern LLM uses. The change is one line; the consequence is structural.

<h2 id="math">Where the LayerNorm goes</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\text{post-LN:}\quad x \leftarrow \text{LN}\big(x + \text{Sublayer}(x)\big)" />

<MathBlock tex="\text{pre-LN:}\quad x \leftarrow x + \text{Sublayer}\big(\text{LN}(x)\big)" />

The difference is what happens to the **residual stream** — the `x` carried forward from block
to block. Under post-LN it passes *through* a LayerNorm at every block, so it is renormalized
every layer. Under pre-LN the stream itself is never normalized; only the copy fed into the
sublayer is. That leaves an unbroken identity path from the model's input all the way to its
output.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why an unbroken identity path matters">
  Month 6's vanishing-gradient lesson showed what repeated multiplication does to a signal
  travelling through many steps. A residual connection is valuable precisely because it offers a
  path with *derivative exactly 1* — nothing multiplies it down or up. Post-LN interrupts that
  path with a normalization at every single block; pre-LN preserves it intact through the whole
  stack.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Read the two measurements together — they say different things">
  The forward-pass table makes post-LN look *better behaved*: its residual stream sits at exactly
  `8.000` at every depth while pre-LN's drifts up to `49.4`. Taken alone that reads like an
  argument for post-LN.

  The sensitivity table reverses the verdict. What matters for training is not whether
  activations look tidy but how strongly the network amplifies a perturbation from input to
  output — because that is the path gradients travel. There, post-LN grows explosively
  (`2.4×10⁵`) while pre-LN stays moderate (`1.4×10²`), a **1318×** gap by depth 40. Neat-looking
  activations and trainability are not the same property, and only the second one determines
  whether a deep stack converges.
</Callout>

## Summary

Post-LN normalizes after the residual add (`LN(x + f(x))`), pre-LN normalizes inside the branch
(`x + f(LN(x))`). Post-LN pins the residual stream to a constant magnitude but interrupts the
identity path at every block, and its input-to-output sensitivity grows explosively with depth —
`2.4×10⁵` over 40 layers versus pre-LN's `1.4×10²`, a 1318× gap. That runaway sensitivity, not
activation size, is what made deep post-LN stacks require careful learning-rate warmup, and why
pre-LN is now standard. The next lesson looks at weight tying and where a GPT's parameters
actually go.
