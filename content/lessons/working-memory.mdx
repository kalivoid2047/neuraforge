<h2 id="intuition">Every strategy fits the budget; they do not all remember</h2>

Week one showed the transcript growing without bound. Three strategies keep it inside a context budget, and comparing them on token count alone makes them look interchangeable. Comparing them on what they still *know* does not.

<h2 id="math">Three Ways to Stay Under Budget</h2>

- **Grow forever** — keep everything. Exact, until it exceeds the window and the run dies.
- **Sliding window** — keep the most recent `k` tokens. Cheap, `O(1)` memory, and drops old content with no idea whether it mattered.
- **Summarization** — compress older steps into a digest, keep recent ones verbatim.

The measurement below runs all three on the same 40-step task with a 2,000-token budget, then asks the question that actually matters: which of the five fact-carrying steps does each one still have?

<Callout kind="danger" title="A recency-only summarizer retained fewer facts than plain truncation">
  This is the result worth remembering. The sliding window kept **2 of 5** needed facts. A summarizer that compresses by recency — with no notion of which steps carry information — kept **1 of 5**, *worse than simply truncating*. Only the fact-aware version kept 5 of 5, and it did so because an extraction rule was written by hand. Summarizing is not remembering. If nothing in your pipeline decides what is worth keeping, compression will discard it just as readily as truncation, while costing an extra model call for the privilege.
</Callout>

## Summary

Unbounded growth is exact until it kills the run; sliding windows are cheap and blind; summarization is only as good as its retention rule. Measured on the same 40-step task, the window kept **2 of 5** needed facts, a recency-only summarizer kept **1 of 5** — worse than truncation — and only the fact-aware version kept all five. Compression is not memory. Writing the rule that decides what matters is the work.
