<h2 id="intuition">People are bad at scoring and good at comparing</h2>

SFT teaches a model to imitate demonstrations, which caps it at demonstration quality and cannot express *preferences between* acceptable answers. The next stage needs a signal for "this response is better than that one" — and the practical way to collect that is not to ask people for scores. Asked to rate a response 1-10, annotators are inconsistent with each other and with themselves. Asked which of two is better, they are far more reliable.

<h2 id="math">Learning From Comparisons</h2>

The **Bradley-Terry** model turns comparisons into a learnable scalar reward:

<MathBlock tex="P(y_c \succ y_r \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_c) - r(x,y_r)\big)" />

Maximizing the likelihood of observed comparisons trains `r` even though `r` was never directly observed. Note the form: only the **difference** of rewards appears.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Reward is only defined up to a constant">
  Because only differences enter the objective, adding any constant to every reward leaves every prediction unchanged — verified below at `3.11e-15`. A reward of 4.2 is meaningless in isolation; it means something only relative to other rewards from the *same* model. This is why you cannot compare raw reward numbers between training runs, and why reward-model quality is reported as pairwise accuracy rather than as an average score.
</Callout>

## Summary

Preference data is collected as pairwise comparisons because people compare far more reliably than they score. Bradley-Terry converts those comparisons into a scalar reward model via `P(chosen ≻ rejected) = σ(r_c − r_r)`, which recovered the true preference direction to cosine **0.9971** from comparisons alone, reaching 97.3% pairwise accuracy. Because only differences appear, reward is identified only up to an additive constant — shifting every reward by 100 changed predictions by `3.11e-15` — so raw reward values are never comparable across runs. The next lesson uses this reward to actually change a policy.
