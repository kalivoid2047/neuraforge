<h2 id="intuition">Half the bits, most of the result</h2>

Every number in Months 3–7 was a 32-bit float. Modern training uses 16-bit floats for most
arithmetic, because GPU tensor cores multiply them several times faster. The catch is that 16
bits is not much: `float16` tops out around `65,504` and, more dangerously, loses very small
numbers to zero — and gradients late in training are *routinely* very small.

<h2 id="math">Three number formats</h2>

A float splits its bits into an **exponent** (range) and a **mantissa** (precision):

<MathBlock tex="\texttt{float32} = 1 + 8_{\text{exp}} + 23_{\text{mant}} \qquad \texttt{float16} = 1 + 5_{\text{exp}} + 10_{\text{mant}} \qquad \texttt{bfloat16} = 1 + 8_{\text{exp}} + 7_{\text{mant}}" />

`bfloat16` makes the more useful trade for deep learning: it keeps float32's **full 8-bit
exponent** — so it never overflows or underflows where float32 wouldn't — and sacrifices
mantissa bits instead. Neural network training tolerates coarse precision far better than it
tolerates values silently becoming zero or infinity, which is why bf16 has largely displaced
fp16 on hardware that supports it, and why bf16 needs no loss scaling.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Loss scaling, in one line">
  If gradients are too small for fp16, multiply the *loss* by a large constant `S` before
  calling backward. Every gradient is scaled by `S` too (the chain rule is linear), lifting them
  into fp16's representable range. Divide by `S` before the optimizer step and the update is
  unchanged. Real implementations pick `S` dynamically: raise it while gradients stay finite,
  halve it whenever an overflow appears.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Mixed precision does not halve your parameter memory">
  It is widely assumed that "16-bit training halves memory." On the parameter side that is
  **false**, and the arithmetic above shows exactly why: mixed precision keeps an fp32 *master
  copy* of the weights (fp16 weights lose too much precision to accumulate small updates
  reliably), and Adam's two moment buffers stay fp32 as well. Those additions cancel the halved
  weights and gradients precisely — `16` bytes per parameter either way.

  The genuine benefits are activation memory, which does roughly halve, and throughput, which is
  the real reason for adopting it. If your model does not fit in memory, mixed precision alone
  will not fix that — gradient checkpointing (8.2.4) and sharding (8.2.5) will.
</Callout>

## Summary

`float16` has a 5-bit exponent that silently flushes small gradients to zero — verified above,
where `1e-8` and `1e-9` both became exactly `0.0`. Loss scaling multiplies the loss by a
constant before backward and divides it out afterward, recovering every underflowed gradient in
the test. `bfloat16` avoids the problem entirely by keeping float32's 8-bit exponent at the cost
of mantissa precision, which is why it needs no scaling. And the common "16-bit halves memory"
belief is wrong on the parameter side: with fp32 master weights and fp32 Adam state, the total
is 16 bytes per parameter either way. The next lesson covers gradient accumulation, which does
address batch size directly.
