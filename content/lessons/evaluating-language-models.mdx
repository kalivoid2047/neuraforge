<h2 id="intuition">The metric everyone quotes has a trap in it</h2>

Month 6 defined perplexity as exponentiated cross-entropy and used it to compare models. That comparison is only valid under a condition rarely stated aloud: **the models must share a tokenizer**. Perplexity is per-*token*, so a tokenizer that splits text into more pieces spreads the same total uncertainty across more predictions and reports a lower number — for an identical model on identical text.

<h2 id="math">Perplexity Is Not Comparable Across Tokenizers</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\text{PPL} = \exp\!\left(\frac{\text{NLL}_{\text{total}}}{N_{\text{tokens}}}\right) \qquad \text{BPB} = \frac{\text{NLL}_{\text{total}}}{\ln 2 \cdot N_{\text{bytes}}}" />

Perplexity normalizes by token count, which the tokenizer controls. Bits-per-byte normalizes by **bytes**, which it cannot.

<Callout kind="tip" title="What perplexity cannot tell you">
  Even within one tokenizer, low perplexity does not imply a model is useful. It measures next-token prediction on held-out text and nothing else — not instruction-following, not factual accuracy, not reasoning. A model can have excellent perplexity and still be unhelpful, which is exactly why Month 9's alignment work exists and why downstream benchmarks are reported alongside loss rather than instead of it.
</Callout>

## Summary

Perplexity is per-token, so the same model on the same text reported `12.18` under a 100-token segmentation and `5.96` under a 140-token one — a 2.04x difference caused entirely by tokenization. Bits-per-byte normalizes by bytes instead and returned an identical `0.7514` for both, which is why cross-tokenizer comparisons use it. Neither metric measures usefulness: low loss on held-out text says nothing about instruction-following or factual accuracy. The next lesson covers diagnosing a run that is not converging.
