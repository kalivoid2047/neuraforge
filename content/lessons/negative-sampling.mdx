<h2 id="intuition">The softmax denominator is the bottleneck</h2>

Skip-gram's objective needs `P(\text{context} \mid \text{center})`, which — computed with the
softmax from Month 5 — requires summing `e^{z_j}` over **every word in the vocabulary** just to
get the denominator. For a 100,000-word vocabulary, that's 100,000 operations per training pair,
and there may be billions of pairs. **Negative sampling** replaces this expensive multi-class
problem with a much cheaper *binary* one: instead of "which of 100,000 words is the context?",
ask "is this specific (center, context) pair real, or fake?" — and train on the one real pair
plus just a handful of randomly-drawn fake ones.

<h2 id="math">Replacing the softmax</h2>

For a real (center `c`, context `o`) pair and `k` sampled negative words `n_i`:

<MathBlock tex="\log \sigma(v_o \cdot v_c) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} \log \sigma(-v_{n_i} \cdot v_c)" />

where `σ` is the sigmoid (Month 4). The first term pushes real pairs' embeddings toward higher
dot product; each negative term pushes randomly-paired embeddings apart. With `k` typically 5–20,
this costs `k+1` operations instead of the full vocabulary — a several-thousand-fold reduction.

Negatives aren't drawn uniformly, nor by raw frequency, but from a **`3/4`-power distribution**:

<MathBlock tex="P_n(w) = \frac{\text{count}(w)^{3/4}}{\sum_{w'} \text{count}(w')^{3/4}}" />

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why the 3/4 power specifically -- and how big the effect really is">
  The `3/4` exponent sits between raw-frequency sampling (which over-draws corpus-dominating
  words like "the") and uniform sampling (which over-draws extremely rare ones). It *compresses*
  the range without flattening it. Worth noting from the numbers below: the effect is **strongly
  asymmetric**. Very frequent words barely move (in the example corpus, "the" only drops from
  45.8% to 40.2% of draws), while rare words gain a lot in relative terms — "lachrymose" becomes
  roughly **7x** more likely to be sampled than under raw frequency. The main practical benefit
  is lifting rare words out of near-never-sampled territory, not dramatically suppressing common
  ones. The exponent was found empirically in the original paper — a practical tuning result, not
  a theoretical derivation.
</Callout>

## Summary

Negative sampling replaces skip-gram's expensive full softmax — which requires summing over the
entire vocabulary per training pair — with a cheap binary discrimination task: distinguish one
real (center, context) pair from `k` randomly sampled fake ones. Negatives are drawn from a
`3/4`-power frequency distribution that compresses the range between very common and very rare
words — an effect that turns out to be strongly asymmetric when measured: very frequent words
are suppressed only modestly, while rare words become several times more likely to be sampled,
verified above against uniform and raw-frequency sampling with empirical draw frequencies
confirming the computed distribution. The next lesson covers GloVe, which reaches
similar embeddings from an entirely different starting point: factorizing the co-occurrence
matrix directly.
