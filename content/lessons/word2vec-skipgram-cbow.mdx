<h2 id="intuition">Learning embeddings by solving a fake prediction task</h2>

Co-occurrence matrices (last lesson) capture distributional information but are huge and sparse.
**Word2Vec** gets dense embeddings by a clever trick: set up a *prediction task* — predict a
word's context from the word, or vice versa — train a small network on it, then **throw the
prediction task away and keep the network's internal weight matrix**, which turns out to be
exactly a table of dense word embeddings. The prediction task is never the point; it's a
scaffold that forces the network to learn useful representations.

<h2 id="math">Two prediction tasks</h2>

**Skip-gram**: given a center word, predict its surrounding context words.

<MathBlock tex="\max \sum_{\text{center}} \sum_{\text{context} \in \text{window}} \log P(\text{context} \mid \text{center})" />

**CBOW (Continuous Bag of Words)**: the reverse — given the surrounding context words, predict
the center word.

<MathBlock tex="\max \sum_{\text{center}} \log P(\text{center} \mid \text{context words})" />

Both train an embedding matrix `E` of shape `(vocab_size, embedding_dim)`. Row `i` of `E` is
word `i`'s dense embedding — and *that matrix* is the deliverable, extracted and used elsewhere
long after the prediction task is discarded.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Skip-gram vs. CBOW, practically">
  CBOW is faster to train and tends to work better for frequent words (it averages several
  context words into one prediction, which smooths noise). Skip-gram is slower but generally
  produces better embeddings for *rare* words, since each individual (center, context) pair
  becomes its own training example rather than being averaged away. Skip-gram with negative
  sampling (next lesson) is the more commonly used combination in practice.
</Callout>

## Summary

Word2Vec learns dense embeddings by training on a proxy prediction task — skip-gram predicts
context words from a center word, CBOW predicts the center word from its context — and then
discarding the predictions entirely, keeping only the learned embedding matrix. Skip-gram
generates far more individual training examples per sentence than CBOW (verified above: 30 vs. 9
from one nine-word sentence), which is exactly why it trains more slowly but handles rare words
better. The next lesson covers negative sampling, the optimization that makes training this
efficiently over a large vocabulary actually feasible.
