<h2 id="intuition">Safety training is easier to remove than to install</h2>

The uncomfortable finding of the last two lessons is that safety behaviour degrades under fine-tuning that has nothing to do with safety. Training on SQL examples can measurably reduce a model's refusal rate on harmful requests. Nobody asked for that; it happens because alignment lives in the same weights everything else does.

<h2 id="math">Alignment Is Fragile</h2>

The mechanism is week one's catastrophic forgetting applied to alignment. Refusal behaviour was installed by SFT and preference training; a subsequent fine-tune optimizes a loss containing no term for it:

<MathBlock tex="\theta_{\text{final}} = \arg\min_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta) \quad\text{with no term for refusal behaviour}" />

Weights that encoded refusal are free to move, and gradient descent has no reason to preserve them. This is not adversarial — it happens with entirely benign training data.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Static benchmarks measure the attacks you already thought of">
  A fixed safety benchmark tests a known list of harmful prompts, so a model can score well on it and still fail against phrasings the list does not contain. **Red-teaming** — having people or models actively search for failures — finds what the benchmark cannot, precisely because it is adaptive. Both are needed: the benchmark for regression tracking across versions, red-teaming for discovering what should be on the benchmark next.
</Callout>

## Summary

Safety behaviour erodes under fine-tuning that never mentions safety, because the fine-tuning objective contains no term for it and the weights encoding refusal are free to move — week one's catastrophic forgetting, applied to alignment. This occurs with benign data, which is what makes it easy to miss. Static benchmarks track regressions against known prompts; red-teaming adaptively searches for failures the benchmark does not contain, and both are needed. A large safety regression is a deployment blocker rather than a note in the appendix. The capstone assembles the full report.
