<h2 id="intuition">Closing week two: the same destination, a different road</h2>

Word2Vec learns embeddings by sliding a window across text, one local context at a time, never
looking at the corpus as a whole. **GloVe** (Global Vectors) takes the opposite approach: build
the full co-occurrence matrix first (exactly the one from lesson 6.2.2), then directly factorize
it into dense vectors. Its key insight is that the meaningful signal lives not in raw
co-occurrence probabilities, but in their **ratios**.

<h2 id="math">Ratios, not raw probabilities</h2>

Consider the words `ice` and `steam`, and probe words `k`. Looking at the ratio:

<MathBlock tex="\frac{P(k \mid \text{ice})}{P(k \mid \text{steam})}" />

- For `k = solid` (related to ice, not steam), the ratio is **large**
- For `k = gas` (related to steam, not ice), the ratio is **small**
- For `k = water` (related to both) or `k = fashion` (related to neither), the ratio is **near 1**

The ratio cleanly separates "discriminating" probe words from non-discriminating ones, in a way
raw probabilities alone don't — `P(water|ice)` and `P(fashion|ice)` might both be unremarkable
numbers individually, but their *ratios* against steam behave very differently. GloVe's training
objective is built to make embedding dot products reproduce the log of these co-occurrence
counts:

<MathBlock tex="w_i \cdot \tilde{w}_j + b_i + \tilde{b}_j \approx \log C_{ij}" />

<Callout kind="tip" title="Two routes to remarkably similar destinations">
  Word2Vec (local windows, predictive) and GloVe (global matrix, count-based) come from
  genuinely different traditions and were argued about extensively when GloVe was published. In
  practice the embeddings they produce perform comparably on most tasks — and later theoretical
  work showed skip-gram with negative sampling is *implicitly* factorizing a (shifted PMI)
  co-occurrence matrix, meaning the two approaches are far more closely related than they first
  appear.
</Callout>

## Week two summary

This week built word embeddings from the ground up: one-hot vectors encode identity but no
similarity whatsoever; the distributional hypothesis makes similarity learnable from raw
unlabeled text via co-occurrence statistics; Word2Vec's skip-gram and CBOW learn dense
embeddings through a throwaway prediction task; negative sampling makes that training tractable
over large vocabularies; and GloVe reaches comparable embeddings from the opposite direction, by
factorizing the global co-occurrence matrix with an objective built around co-occurrence ratios.
Week three turns to language modeling itself — using representations like these to predict what
word comes next.

## Further reading
