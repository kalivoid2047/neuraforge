<h2 id="intuition">Picking the wrong similarity silently ranks long documents first</h2>

Once documents are vectors, ranking needs a similarity measure. The three candidates are not interchangeable, and the difference between them is not subtle: one of them can be gamed by simply repeating your text.

<h2 id="math">Three Similarity Measures</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\text{dot}(a,b)=a\cdot b \qquad \cos(a,b)=\frac{a\cdot b}{\|a\|\|b\|} \qquad d_2(a,b)=\|a-b\|" />

Cosine measures the **angle** and ignores magnitude. Dot product conflates angle with magnitude. And on unit-length vectors the three collapse into one:

<MathBlock tex="\|a\|=\|b\|=1 \;\Longrightarrow\; \cos(a,b)=a\cdot b \quad\text{and}\quad \|a-b\|^2 = 2-2(a\cdot b)" />

So normalizing once at index time lets you use the fastest operation (dot product) while getting cosine's semantics for free — which is why vector databases store unit vectors.

<Callout kind="warning" title="The padding attack">
  The test below is deliberately adversarial: take a document and repeat it verbatim three times. It is no more relevant, only longer. Raw dot product rises by exactly **3.00×**. Cosine changes by **0.00e+00** — mathematically unchanged, because repetition scales the vector without rotating it. Any ranking built on unnormalized dot products can be inflated by padding.
</Callout>

## Summary

Cosine measures angle, dot product conflates angle with magnitude, and Euclidean distance is a monotone function of cosine once vectors are unit length — so vector databases normalize at index time and use dot products. The padding test makes the stakes concrete: repeating a document verbatim inflated its raw dot product by exactly `3.00×` while its cosine changed by `0.00e+00`. The next lesson asks what happens when there are a billion vectors to compare against.
