<h2 id="intuition">A thousand good examples beat a hundred thousand mediocre ones</h2>

Instruction tuning does not teach a model facts — pretraining did that. It teaches **format and behaviour**: answer the question, adopt this register, structure the response this way. That is a much smaller thing to learn, which is why curated sets of a few thousand examples routinely outperform scraped sets a hundred times larger.

<h2 id="math">What SFT Actually Teaches</h2>

The mechanism is direct: SFT maximizes the likelihood of the demonstrations, so the model learns to imitate them — including their flaws:

<MathBlock tex="\mathcal{L} = -\sum_{t \in \text{assistant}} \log P(w_t \mid w_{<t})" />

If the demonstrations hedge, the model learns to hedge. If they are truncated, it learns to stop early. If they confidently assert wrong facts, it learns that confident tone and applies it to *other* wrong facts. There is no filter that keeps the good parts.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Concrete curation checks">
  **Deduplicate** — near-duplicates waste capacity and skew the distribution. **Decontaminate** — remove benchmark overlap before training, since afterwards is too late. **Length-check** — truncated responses teach the model to stop mid-answer. **Verify a sample by hand** — a few hundred examples read carefully will surface systematic problems that no automated filter catches. **Check refusal coverage** — if no example in the set declines a request, the model learns that declining is never appropriate.
</Callout>

## Summary

Instruction tuning teaches format and behaviour by imitation, so the model absorbs demonstrations' flaws as readily as their strengths — hedging, truncation, and confidently-stated errors are all learned. That is why a few thousand carefully curated examples routinely beat scraped sets orders of magnitude larger. Practical curation means deduplicating, decontaminating before training rather than after, length-checking, reading a sample by hand, and confirming the set contains examples of appropriately declining.
