<h2 id="intuition">The month's argument, tested</h2>

Week one claimed full fine-tuning damages the model. Week two claimed LoRA avoids that damage at a fraction of the cost. Week four claimed you cannot tell without measuring retained capability. This capstone tests all three at once: pretrain a model, adapt it two ways, and produce the three-axis report.

<h2 id="math">The Comparison That Settles It</h2>

The retention test is the crucial piece. Both methods retrain the output head, so both lose the original task's readout — measuring that alone would make them look identical. Instead, freeze each adapted backbone and fit a **fresh head** on the original task:

<MathBlock tex="\text{retained} = \max_{H} \; \text{acc}\big(\text{task A}; \; \text{backbone}_{\text{adapted}},\, H\big)" />

This measures whether the *features* survived, which is the damage that actually matters.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why LoRA's retention is exactly 1.000, not merely high">
  LoRA's base weights are never updated — not approximately preserved, but **bit-identical** to the pretrained model. So refitting a head on them recovers the original task perfectly, by construction. Full fine-tuning reached `0.880`, and that shortfall is permanent: the features themselves moved, and no amount of head-refitting recovers what they encoded.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="58.7% trainable is an artifact of the model's size — read the breakdown">
  LoRA used 58.7% of the base model's parameters here, which sounds like a poor result against the
  **0.0626%** computed for a 7B model in lesson 9.2.2. The breakdown explains it: the adapter
  itself is 176 parameters while the output head is 165, and on a 581-parameter model the head is
  enormous in relative terms.

  On a real model the head is negligible and the adapter sets the cost. Small-model demonstrations
  systematically understate LoRA's efficiency, and quoting 58.7% as LoRA's overhead would be
  straightforwardly misleading.
</Callout>

## Month 9 summary

**Week one** established the problem: full fine-tuning adapts well and damages what it does not measure, taking a pretrained task from 1.000 to 0.172 while degrading the underlying features. **Week two** solved it — PEFT freezes the base and trains added parameters, cutting a 7B fine-tune from 112 GB to 14.1 GB, with LoRA's adapter merging back exactly (`1.3e-15`) and NF4 quantization proving `1.76×` more accurate than uniform int4 to reach 3.6 GB under QLoRA. **Week three** covered what to train on: chat templates with assistant-only loss masking, reward models recovering human preference direction to cosine `0.9971` from comparisons alone, RLHF's KL constraint against reward hacking, and DPO eliminating the reward model entirely by exploiting the very shift-invariance that made reward unidentifiable. **Week four** insisted on measuring all of it. This capstone closes the argument: LoRA matched full fine-tuning on the target task (both 1.000) while retaining `1.000` against full fine-tuning's `0.880` — a difference invisible to any report that measures only what it set out to improve.

Month 10 turns from changing a model's weights to changing what it can see.
