<h2 id="intuition">Closing week one: proving the gradient's "steepest ascent" claim</h2>

Last lesson asserted the gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, without proof.
This closing lesson proves it — using the **directional derivative**, which generalizes "rate
of change" to *any* direction, not just along a single axis.

<h2 id="math">The directional derivative formula</h2>

The rate of change of `f` at point `p`, moving in unit direction `û`:

<MathBlock tex="D_{\hat{u}}f(p) = \nabla f(p) \cdot \hat{u}" />

Just the dot product of the gradient with the direction — Month 1's tool, doing real work
again. Since `a·b = |a||b|cos(θ)` (Month 1), and `û` has length 1:

<MathBlock tex="D_{\hat{u}}f(p) = |\nabla f(p)|\cos\theta" />

This is maximized exactly when `cos(θ) = 1`, i.e. `θ = 0` — when `û` points in **exactly the
same direction as the gradient**. That's the proof: the gradient direction is, by construction
of this formula, the direction that maximizes the rate of increase. No direction gives a faster
rate of ascent than the gradient's own direction.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Directly explaining gradient descent, one lesson early">
  If the gradient's OWN direction maximizes the rate of *increase*, then the *negative* gradient
  direction maximizes the rate of *decrease* — the fastest way to reduce a loss function. That's
  the entire idea behind gradient descent, formalized properly in week four. Everything week one
  built (derivatives → partials → gradient → this proof) leads directly to that one algorithm.
</Callout>

## Summary

The directional derivative `D_u f(p) = ∇f(p)·u` measures the rate of change of `f` along any
direction `u`, generalizing single-axis partial derivatives to arbitrary directions. Because it
equals `|∇f(p)|cos(θ)`, it's maximized exactly when `u` aligns with the gradient — proving the
gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its negation points in the direction of
steepest descent. This closes week one; week two builds the chain rule needed to compute
gradients through composed, multi-layer functions like a neural network.
