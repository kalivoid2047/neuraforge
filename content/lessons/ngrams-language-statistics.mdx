<h2 id="intuition">Recovering a little bit of word order</h2>

Bag-of-words and TF-IDF both discard word order entirely. **N-grams** — contiguous sequences of
`n` tokens — recover a limited, local amount of it: a **bigram** (`n=2`) captures which words
tend to follow which; a **trigram** (`n=3`) captures slightly longer local patterns. Counting
n-grams across a corpus, and turning those counts into conditional probabilities, is exactly the
technique behind the earliest statistical language models — and directly reuses week one's
conditional probability, now applied to word sequences instead of dice or coins.

<h2 id="math">N-grams as conditional probability</h2>

A bigram model estimates the probability of a word given the *one* word before it, using
observed counts (an application of the MLE principle from week two, applied to language):

<MathBlock tex="P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(w_{i-1}, w_i)}{\text{count}(w_{i-1})}" />

This is the same "observed frequency" idea behind the Bernoulli MLE (`k/n`) from two lessons
ago — just counting pairs of adjacent words instead of individual coin flips.

<Callout kind="tip" title="What n-grams still can't capture">
  A bigram model only ever looks *one word back* — it has no memory of anything earlier in the
  sentence. `"The cat that chased the mouse was hungry"` needs to connect `"cat"` (the subject)
  to `"was"` (its verb), five words later — completely outside a bigram's one-word window. This
  specific limitation (fixed, short, local context) is exactly what motivates the recurrent and
  attention-based models covered starting Month 6 and Month 7.
</Callout>

<h2 id="python">Bigram counts and conditional probabilities, computed directly</h2>

<CodeCell
  code={"import re\nfrom collections import defaultdict\n\ndef tokenize(text):\n    return [tok for tok in re.split(r'[^a-zA-Z]+', text.lower()) if tok]\n\ncorpus = [\n    \"the cat sat on the mat\",\n    \"the cat sat on the log\",\n    \"the dog ran in the park\",\n    \"the cat ran in the park\",\n]\n\n# extract all bigrams (adjacent word pairs) across the corpus\nbigram_counts = defaultdict(int)\nunigram_counts = defaultdict(int)\nfor doc in corpus:\n    tokens = tokenize(doc)\n    for word in tokens:\n        unigram_counts[word] += 1\n    for i in range(len(tokens) - 1):\n        bigram_counts[(tokens[i], tokens[i+1])] += 1\n\nprint(\"a few bigram counts:\")\nfor bigram in [(\"the\", \"cat\"), (\"the\", \"dog\"), (\"cat\", \"sat\"), (\"cat\", \"ran\")]:\n    print(f\"  {bigram}: {bigram_counts[bigram]}\")\nprint()\n\ndef bigram_probability(w_prev, w_curr):\n    return bigram_counts[(w_prev, w_curr)] / unigram_counts[w_prev]\n\nprint(\"P(next word | 'the'):\")\nfor word in [\"cat\", \"dog\", \"mat\"]:\n    print(f\"  P({word} | the) = {bigram_probability('the', word):.4f}\")\n\nprint()\nprint(\"P(next word | 'cat'):\")\nfor word in [\"sat\", \"ran\"]:\n    print(f\"  P({word} | cat) = {bigram_probability('cat', word):.4f}\")\n\n# verify: probabilities of ALL words following 'the' should sum to 1\nfollowers_of_the = [w for (w_prev, w) in bigram_counts if w_prev == \"the\"]\ntotal_prob = sum(bigram_probability(\"the\", w) for w in followers_of_the)\nprint()\nprint(f\"sum of P(w | the) over all observed followers of 'the': {total_prob:.4f}\")"}

## Summary

N-grams recover a small, local amount of word-order information that bag-of-words and TF-IDF
discard, estimating `P(w_i | w_{i-1})` directly from observed adjacent-word counts — the same
MLE "observed frequency" principle from week two, now applied to word pairs, verified directly
above. Their fixed, one-word (or few-word) context window is a real limitation for
longer-range dependencies, motivating the sequence models covered in later months. The final
lesson this week builds the Naive Bayes classifier, combining Bayes' theorem (week one) with the
bag-of-words representation (this week) into an actual working text classifier.
