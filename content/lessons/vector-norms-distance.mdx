<h2 id="intuition">"How big is this vector?" has more than one honest answer</h2>

A vector's **norm** is a measure of its size/length — but which notion of "size" you want
depends on context, and different norms give genuinely different answers, not just different
units. The two you'll meet constantly: **L2** (ordinary straight-line length) and **L1** (sum of
absolute values, "taxicab distance").

<h2 id="math">L2 and L1, defined</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\|\vec{v}\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum_i v_i^2} \qquad \text{(Euclidean norm --- straight-line length)}" />
<MathBlock tex="\|\vec{v}\|_1 = \sum_i |v_i| \qquad \text{(Manhattan/taxicab norm --- sum of absolute values)}" />

L2 is exactly the Pythagorean theorem, generalized past 2D: for `v = [3, 4]`,
`‖v‖₂ = √(3² + 4²) = √25 = 5` — the length of the hypotenuse. L1 answers a different question:
if you could only move parallel to the axes (like navigating city blocks, hence "taxicab"), how
far would you travel? For the same vector, `‖v‖₁ = |3| + |4| = 7` — more than the straight-line
distance, because it can't cut diagonally.

<h2 id="python">Computing both, and distance between two points</h2>

<CodeCell
  code={"import numpy as np\n\nv = np.array([3, 4])\n\nl2 = np.sqrt(np.sum(v ** 2))\nl2_builtin = np.linalg.norm(v)               # default is L2\nl1 = np.sum(np.abs(v))\nl1_builtin = np.linalg.norm(v, ord=1)\n\nprint(f\"L2 norm (manual):  {l2}\")\nprint(f\"L2 norm (builtin): {l2_builtin}\")\nprint(f\"L1 norm (manual):  {l1}\")\nprint(f\"L1 norm (builtin): {l1_builtin}\")\n\n# distance between two points = norm of their difference\npoint_a = np.array([1, 1])\npoint_b = np.array([4, 5])\ndifference = point_b - point_a\neuclidean_distance = np.linalg.norm(difference)\nprint()\nprint(\"point_a:\", point_a, \" point_b:\", point_b)\nprint(\"difference vector:\", difference)\nprint(\"Euclidean distance between them:\", euclidean_distance)"}
  output={"L2 norm (manual):  5.0\nL2 norm (builtin): 5.0\nL1 norm (manual):  7\nL1 norm (builtin): 7.0\n\npoint_a: [1 1]  point_b: [4 5]\ndifference vector: [3 4]\nEuclidean distance between them: 5.0"}
/>

<Callout kind="tip" title="Distance is just 'norm of the difference'">
  This is a pattern worth internalizing early: "distance between two points" is always `‖a −
  b‖` for whichever norm you care about. There's no separate distance formula to memorize —
  it's the norm you already know, applied to the difference vector.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Why the choice of norm actually matters">
  L1 and L2 aren't just different numbers for the same idea — they behave differently under
  optimization. L1 tends to push small values all the way to exactly zero (useful for encouraging
  "sparse" solutions — few nonzero parameters); L2 shrinks values smoothly toward zero without
  usually reaching it exactly. You'll meet this distinction again as L1/L2 *regularization* when
  training models later in this course.
</Callout>

## Summary

A vector's norm measures its size, but "size" has multiple honest definitions: L2 (Euclidean,
straight-line length, the Pythagorean theorem generalized) and L1 (Manhattan, sum of absolute
values, axis-constrained travel). Distance between two points is always the norm of their
difference vector — one formula, reused with whichever norm fits the situation. The choice
between L1 and L2 has real downstream consequences, seen again later as regularization.
