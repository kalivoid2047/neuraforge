<h2 id="intuition">Closing week one: when outcomes aren't a countable list</h2>

A die roll has 6 possible outcomes; a coin flip has 2. But height, temperature, and (crucially
for later this month) the weights inside a neural network can take *any* real value in some
range — there are infinitely many possible outcomes, so no single outcome can have positive
probability (`P(\text{height}=170.00000\ldots\text{cm})=0` exactly, for any exact value). A
**probability density function (PDF)** handles this: instead of assigning probability to exact
points, it describes probability per unit length, and actual probabilities come from the *area*
under the curve over some range.

<h2 id="math">Density instead of mass</h2>

The **Gaussian (Normal) distribution**, by far the most important continuous distribution in
practice:

<MathBlock tex="f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)" />

`μ` (mean) sets the center of the bell curve; `σ²` (variance) sets its width. Unlike a PMF, `f(x)`
itself is **not** a probability — it's a density, and can even exceed `1`. The actual
probability of landing in some range `[a,b]` is the *integral* (the area under the curve) of
`f(x)` from `a` to `b`.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why the Gaussian shows up everywhere">
  Beyond describing naturally-occurring measurements, the Gaussian is the limiting distribution
  the Central Limit Theorem (next lesson) predicts for *sums of many independent random
  effects* — which is why it appears so often as a reasonable modeling assumption, and why it's
  used deliberately as the initialization distribution for neural network weights (recall
  `rng.normal(...)` initializing weights throughout Months 3 and 4).
</Callout>

## Summary

Continuous random variables need a probability density function rather than a PMF, because any
single exact value has probability zero — actual probabilities come from the area under the
density curve over a range. The Gaussian distribution, `f(x) = (1/√(2πσ²))·exp(-(x-μ)²/2σ²)`,
was verified above by matching its exact formula against a histogram of two million simulated
samples, confirming both the shape and the classic "68% within one standard deviation" rule.
This closes week one's probability foundations; week two turns to statistics — estimating these
distributions' parameters from data, and the theorem that explains why the Gaussian appears so
often in the first place.
