<h2 id="intuition">Most training failures announce themselves in the curve</h2>

Training runs fail in a small number of characteristic ways, and each leaves a distinctive shape in the loss curve. Learning to read those shapes turns debugging from guesswork into a short checklist — and the capstone at the end of this week hit one of these failures for real.

<h2 id="math">Reading a Loss Curve</h2>

There is no formula here; the skill is pattern recognition over five shapes:

- **Healthy** — smooth decay flattening toward a floor.
- **Learning rate too high** — an initial drop, then oscillation that never settles.
- **Learning rate too low** — near-linear, barely descending.
- **Diverged** — loss climbs or becomes `NaN`.
- **Overfitting** — training loss keeps falling while *validation* turns upward.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Two checks before touching any hyperparameter">
  **First, gradient-check on a tiny batch.** A wrong gradient does not crash; it trains badly, and every hour spent tuning the learning rate afterward is wasted. **Second, overfit a single batch to near-zero loss.** A correct model with a working optimizer can always memorize one batch — if it cannot, the bug is in the model or optimizer, not the hyperparameters. Only once both pass is tuning a reasonable use of time.
</Callout>

## Summary

Training failures have recognizable loss-curve signatures: smooth decay is healthy, oscillation means the learning rate is too high, a near-flat line means it is too low, `NaN` means divergence, and rising validation loss against falling training loss means overfitting. Two checks come before any tuning -- gradient-check on a tiny batch, then overfit a single batch to near-zero -- because both eliminate whole classes of cause. The capstone applies exactly this: a passing gradient check plus late-run collapse pointed straight at the optimizer.
