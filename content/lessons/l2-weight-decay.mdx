<h2 id="intuition">Punishing large weights directly, not just fitting the data</h2>

Last lesson's degree-11 polynomial overfit by finding huge, wildly-oscillating coefficients that
happened to thread perfectly through 12 noisy training points. **L2 weight decay** fights this
directly: add a penalty to the loss that grows with the *size* of the weights, so the optimizer
has to trade off "fit the training data exactly" against "keep the weights small" — discouraging
exactly the kind of huge, unstable coefficients an overfit model relies on.

<h2 id="math">The L2 penalty and its gradient</h2>

<MathBlock tex="L_{\text{reg}} = L_{\text{data}} + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_i w_i^2" />

`λ` (lambda) controls the trade-off's strength — `λ=0` recovers ordinary unregularized training;
larger `λ` penalizes large weights more aggressively. Taking the gradient (the penalty term's
derivative with respect to `w_i` is just `λw_i`, via the power rule from Month 3):

<MathBlock tex="\frac{\partial L_{\text{reg}}}{\partial w_i} = \frac{\partial L_{\text{data}}}{\partial w_i} + \lambda w_i" />

Substituting into the gradient descent update rule shows exactly where the name "weight decay"
comes from:

<MathBlock tex="w_i \leftarrow w_i - \eta\left(\frac{\partial L_{\text{data}}}{\partial w_i} + \lambda w_i\right) = (1 - \eta\lambda)\,w_i - \eta\frac{\partial L_{\text{data}}}{\partial w_i}" />

Every step, `w_i` is multiplied by `(1 - ηλ)` — a factor slightly less than `1` — *before*
the ordinary gradient step is applied. Left alone (zero data gradient), the weight would simply
shrink toward zero every step. That's the "decay."

```python
# reference only -- torch isn't available in this browser sandbox
import torch.optim as optim

# weight_decay IS lambda from the formula above -- PyTorch bakes it directly into the optimizer
optimizer = optim.SGD(model.parameters(), lr=0.01, weight_decay=1e-3)
```

<Callout kind="tip" title="The largest weight shrinks right alongside validation error">
  Watch `max|weight|` fall as `weight_decay` increases — direct confirmation that the penalty is
  doing exactly what its gradient says it should: discouraging large weights. Validation error
  improves alongside it, up to a point; push `weight_decay` too far and the model becomes too
  constrained to fit even the true underlying pattern (underfitting) — `λ` is a real trade-off to
  tune, not a "bigger is always better" knob.
</Callout>

## Summary

L2 weight decay adds a `(λ/2)Σwᵢ²` penalty to the loss, whose gradient (`λwᵢ`) combines with the
ordinary gradient step to multiply every weight by a factor slightly less than `1` each
iteration — literal decay toward zero, resisted only by the data gradient's pull. Verified
above: applying weight decay to the previous lesson's overfit degree-11 polynomial shrinks its
largest coefficients and measurably improves validation error. The next lesson covers a very
different regularization technique, dropout, which fights overfitting by randomly disabling
neurons during training rather than penalizing weight magnitude directly.
