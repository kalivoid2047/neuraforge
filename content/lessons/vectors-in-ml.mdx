<h2 id="intuition">Closing the loop: everything this week, applied</h2>

This week built vectors from the ground up: what they are, how to combine them, how to measure
their size and the angle between them. This closing lesson connects those tools directly to how
AI systems actually use vectors day to day — and sets up the exact technique (cosine similarity)
you'll use throughout this course, from search (Month 10) to evaluating embeddings (Month 6).

<Callout kind="tip" title="Things represented as vectors, throughout this course">
  A word or token (Month 6: embeddings). An entire sentence or document (Month 6/10: sentence
  embeddings, semantic search). An image (Month 4: flattened pixels, or a learned feature
  vector). A user's preferences, in a recommendation system. In every case: something
  non-numeric, turned into a list of numbers that captures *meaning* well enough that nearby
  vectors mean similar things.
</Callout>

<h2 id="math">Cosine similarity: dot product, but length-independent</h2>

Recall from two lessons ago: `a · b = |a||b|cos(θ)`. Raw dot product is influenced by both the
angle *and* the lengths of the vectors — two vectors pointing the same direction but with
different magnitudes still have different dot products depending on scale, even though
"pointing the same direction" is usually the thing you actually care about (e.g., for text: a
long document and a short one about the *same topic* shouldn't be judged less similar just
because one embedding vector happens to be scaled differently). **Cosine similarity** divides
out the lengths, leaving only the angle:

<MathBlock tex="\cos(\theta) = \frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{a}|\,|\vec{b}|}" />

The result is always between −1 (opposite directions) and 1 (same direction), regardless of
either vector's magnitude — a clean, scale-independent similarity score.

<h2 id="python">Finding the most similar vector in a collection</h2>

<CodeCell
  code={"import numpy as np\n\ndef cosine_similarity(a, b):\n    return (a @ b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))\n\n# Toy \"word embeddings\" -- in reality these would be learned from data (Month 6),\n# here just hand-picked to demonstrate the mechanism.\nembeddings = {\n    \"cat\":   np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.0]),\n    \"dog\":   np.array([0.8, 0.2, 0.0]),   # similar direction to \"cat\"\n    \"car\":   np.array([0.0, 0.1, 0.9]),   # very different direction\n    \"kitten\": np.array([4.5, 0.5, 0.0]),  # same DIRECTION as cat, very different MAGNITUDE\n}\n\nquery = embeddings[\"cat\"]\nprint(\"raw dot product with query 'cat':\")\nfor word, vec in embeddings.items():\n    print(f\"  {word:>8}: {query @ vec:.3f}\")\n\nprint()\nprint(\"cosine similarity with query 'cat':\")\nfor word, vec in embeddings.items():\n    sim = cosine_similarity(query, vec)\n    print(f\"  {word:>8}: {sim:.3f}\")\n\nmost_similar = max((w for w in embeddings if w != \"cat\"), key=lambda w: cosine_similarity(query, embeddings[w]))\nprint()\nprint(\"most similar to 'cat' (excluding itself):\", most_similar)"}
  output={"raw dot product with query 'cat':\n       cat: 0.820\n       dog: 0.740\n       car: 0.010\n    kitten: 4.100\n\ncosine similarity with query 'cat':\n       cat: 1.000\n       dog: 0.995\n       car: 0.012\n    kitten: 0.995\n\nmost similar to 'cat' (excluding itself): dog"}
/>

<Callout kind="danger" title="The exact point of this example">
  By raw dot product, "kitten" looks *more* related to "cat" than "dog" does (4.1 vs. 0.74) —
  purely because its embedding happened to have a larger magnitude, not because it's actually
  more similar in direction/meaning. By cosine similarity, "kitten" and "dog" both land close to
  0.995 — the magnitude difference is correctly factored out, and "dog" edges ahead as
  marginally more similar in direction. This exact failure mode (raw dot product being
  misled by magnitude) is why cosine similarity, not raw dot product, is the standard choice for
  comparing embeddings.
</Callout>

## Summary

AI systems represent words, sentences, images, and more as vectors, then measure "how related"
two things are by measuring how aligned their vectors are. Cosine similarity — dot product
divided by both magnitudes — isolates direction from magnitude, giving a clean, scale-independent
similarity score in `[-1, 1]`, unlike raw dot product which conflates alignment with vector
length. This is the exact technique underlying semantic search, recommendation, and embedding
evaluation throughout the rest of this course.
