<h2 id="intuition">A canary is a hypothesis test, whether you treat it as one or not</h2>

Canary deploys route a fraction of traffic to a new release and compare error rates. The question almost nobody asks is the one that decides whether the practice works: **how many requests does it take to detect a regression?**

<h2 id="math">How Much Traffic Before You Can Tell?</h2>

Comparing two proportions with significance `α` and power `1-β` requires per arm:

<MathBlock tex="n = \frac{\left(z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{2\bar p(1-\bar p)} + z_{\beta}\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)+p_1(1-p_1)}\right)^2}{(p_1-p_0)^2}" />

The `(p₁-p₀)²` denominator is why small regressions are so expensive to detect, and the numerator is why *rare* events are expensive even when the relative change is large.

<Callout kind="danger" title="Reliability makes regressions harder to see">
  Detecting a doubling from **1% → 2%** needs 2,318 requests per arm. Detecting a doubling from **0.1% → 0.2%** needs **23,510** — ten times more, for the same relative regression. Rare events carry less information per request, so the more reliable your service is, the longer it takes to prove it got worse. Improving your baseline error rate makes your canary weaker, which is genuinely counter-intuitive and rarely accounted for.
</Callout>

## Summary

A canary is a two-proportion hypothesis test. Detecting a 5× or 10× error-rate regression takes minutes and is genuinely useful; detecting a *doubling* of a 0.1% baseline takes **23,510 requests per arm**, or 1.3 hours at 50 rps with 10% canary traffic. And rare events carry less information, so improving your baseline reliability makes regressions harder to detect. A short canary is a smoke test — a valuable one — and calling it validation of subtle regressions is self-deception.
