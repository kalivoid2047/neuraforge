<h2 id="intuition">Identical machinery, one crucial difference</h2>

Full fine-tuning is Month 8's training loop with exactly one change: the weights do not start random. Same forward pass, same cross-entropy loss, same backward pass, same optimizer. That single difference changes everything about how you configure it — most importantly the learning rate, which must be perhaps 10-100x smaller than pretraining's.

<h2 id="math">The Same Loop, Different Starting Point</h2>

The update rule is unchanged from Month 3:

<MathBlock tex="\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{task}}(\theta)" />

What changed is `θ`'s starting value. It begins at a point that already encodes enormous amounts of learned structure, so a large step does not explore — it **destroys**. The gradient of a narrow fine-tuning task points toward that task, and following it far enough will happily walk the weights away from everything else they knew.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why the learning rate drops by one to two orders of magnitude">
  Pretraining starts from noise, where large steps are how you make progress. Fine-tuning starts from a carefully-shaped solution, where large steps are how you leave it. Typical pretraining rates are around `1e-4` to `3e-4`; typical full fine-tuning rates are `1e-5` to `5e-5`. The experiment below uses an aggressive rate deliberately, to make the resulting damage measurable in a few thousand steps.
</Callout>

<h2 id="python">Pretrain on task A, fine-tune on task B, and measure both</h2>

## Summary

Full fine-tuning is Month 8's loop starting from pretrained rather than random weights, which is precisely why the learning rate must fall by one to two orders of magnitude: large steps from a good solution destroy it rather than exploring. The experiment reached 1.000 on the new task while task A collapsed from 1.000 to 0.172 — a result that measuring only the target task would have missed completely. The next lesson examines that collapse directly.
