<h2 id="intuition">'It got slow' is rarely gradual</h2>

Services do not degrade smoothly under load. They are fine, then fine, then suddenly not — and the reason is not a resource running out. It is the queue, and its behaviour is predictable enough to plan against.

<h2 id="math">Why Latency Goes Vertical</h2>

**Little's Law** relates the three quantities that matter:

<MathBlock tex="L = \lambda W" />

Items in the system equal arrival rate times time in system. For a single-server queue with service time `S` and utilization `ρ = λS`:

<MathBlock tex="W = \frac{S}{1-\rho}" />

The `1-ρ` in the denominator is the whole story: as `ρ → 1`, `W → ∞`. Not gradually — hyperbolically.

<Callout kind="danger" title="80% and 90% CPU are on opposite sides of a cliff">
  The measured table shows latency flat from 10% to 50% utilization, then bending, then going vertical. Moving from **80% to 90%** — a 12.5% traffic increase — **doubles** latency, with no code change and nothing exhausted. This is why average CPU is a poor health signal: on a dashboard 80% and 90% look adjacent, and they are on opposite sides of the knee. Plan capacity to roughly **70%** peak; the headroom you appear to waste is what keeps you off the cliff, and it is far cheaper than the incident.
</Callout>

  There is no GPU or web server in this browser, so the numbers below come from an explicit
  cost *model* — stated constants for service time, throughput and price — not from a
  measurement of real hardware. That is the honest framing, and it is also how capacity planning
  is actually done before the hardware is bought. What the cell demonstrates is the **shape** of
  the trade: which way each curve bends, and where it goes vertical. Substitute your own measured
  constants and the conclusions follow the same shape.
</Callout>

## Summary

Little's Law and `W = S/(1-ρ)` explain why services fail suddenly rather than gradually: the `1-ρ` denominator makes latency hyperbolic in utilization. Measured, going from 80% to 90% utilization doubles latency for a 12.5% traffic increase. Plan to ~70% peak. And sizing from a target shows why LLM serving is different — 120 rps of 2-second generation needs 343 workers on a 4-core box, which is a batching problem, not a process-count one.
