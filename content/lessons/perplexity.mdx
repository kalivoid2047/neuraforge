<h2 id="intuition">How surprised is the model, on average?</h2>

Comparing language models needs a single number. Raw sequence probability won't do — longer
sequences always have lower probability, so it can't compare across different test sets.
**Perplexity** normalizes for length and exponentiates, producing a number with a genuinely
intuitive reading: **the effective number of equally-likely choices the model felt it was
choosing between at each token.** A perplexity of 10 means the model was, on average, about as
uncertain as if picking uniformly among 10 options. Lower is better.

<h2 id="math">Perplexity is exponentiated cross-entropy</h2>

For a test sequence of `N` tokens:

<MathBlock tex="\text{PPL} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \log P(w_i \mid \text{context})\right)" />

The expression inside `exp` is exactly the **average negative log-likelihood** — which Month 5,
week two established *is* cross-entropy loss. So perplexity is simply `exp(cross-entropy)`:
the same quantity models are already trained to minimize, re-expressed on a more interpretable
scale.

<Callout kind="tip" title="The uniform-model bound gives perplexity its intuitive meaning">
  A model that assigns *uniform* probability `1/V` to every one of `V` vocabulary words has
  perplexity exactly `V` — it's genuinely choosing uniformly among all `V` options, with no
  information at all. That's the ceiling any useful model must beat. Verified numerically below,
  and it's why "perplexity 30" on a 50,000-word vocabulary means the model has narrowed 50,000
  possibilities down to an effective ~30, a substantial amount of learned structure.
</Callout>

<h2 id="python">Perplexity across models of varying quality, with the uniform bound verified</h2>

## Summary

Perplexity is `exp(cross-entropy)` — the same quantity language models are already trained to
minimize, re-expressed as an "effective number of equally-likely choices per token." A uniform
model over `V` words has perplexity exactly `V`, verified numerically above, which is what gives
the metric its intuitive reading and establishes the baseline any useful model must beat.
Comparing models of varying quality on the same test sequence shows perplexity dropping as the
model assigns more probability to the actual tokens. The next lesson introduces recurrent
neural networks, the first architecture to escape the n-gram sparsity trap.
