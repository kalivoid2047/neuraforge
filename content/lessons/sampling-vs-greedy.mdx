<h2 id="intuition">Week three: the model is fixed — how you read it is a choice</h2>

Training is finished; the weights will not change again. Yet the same model can produce dull
repetitive text or fluent varied text depending entirely on **how you select tokens** from the
distribution it outputs. Month 7 covered greedy and beam search for translation, where there is
roughly one correct answer. Open-ended generation is different, and this week covers the
decoding strategies that difference demands.

<h2 id="math">Two ways to choose</h2>

At each step the model gives a distribution `P(w | context)`. Greedy takes its mode:

<MathBlock tex="w_{\text{greedy}} = \arg\max_w P(w \mid \text{context})" />

Sampling draws from it:

<MathBlock tex="w_{\text{sampled}} \sim P(w \mid \text{context})" />

Greedy is deterministic — identical input always gives identical output. Sampling is stochastic
and reaches every token with nonzero probability.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why maximizing probability produces worse text">
  It is tempting to assume the most probable continuation is the best one. Empirically it is not:
  greedy and beam search on open-ended prompts produce noticeably bland, repetitive text, often
  falling into loops. The reason is that human text is not the maximum-probability sequence —
  real writing constantly makes locally-improbable choices, and a model that never does so reads
  as flat. This is why translation (one target, beam search wins) and open-ended generation
  (many valid targets, sampling wins) use different strategies.
</Callout>

## Summary

Greedy decoding takes the distribution's mode and is fully deterministic — the same five runs
above produced `'the'` every time. Sampling draws from the distribution and faithfully
reproduces it, verified over 200,000 draws. Maximizing probability sounds like it should give
the best text but empirically produces bland, loop-prone output, because human writing is not a
maximum-probability sequence. Pure sampling has the opposite failure: a realistic vocabulary's
long tail contains enough combined mass to derail a generation. The next three lessons fix that
tail — starting with temperature.
