<h2 id="intuition">Week four: deciding how big to build before building it</h2>

The remarkable empirical finding behind modern LLM development is that loss falls as a smooth
**power law** in model size and data — predictably enough that you can estimate a model's final
loss before training it. That predictability is what makes multi-million-dollar training runs
defensible: the outcome is forecast, not gambled on.

It also poses a concrete allocation question. Given a fixed compute budget, is it better to
train a large model on less data, or a smaller model on more? There is a definite answer, and
getting it wrong wastes a substantial fraction of the budget.

<h2 id="math">The power law</h2>

<MathBlock tex="L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}}" />

`N` is parameters, `D` is training tokens. The three terms are meaningfully different: `A/N^α`
is loss from the model being too small to represent the data, `B/D^β` is loss from not having
seen enough data, and **`E` is irreducible** — the intrinsic entropy of language itself. No
amount of scale drives loss below `E`, because some genuine unpredictability in text cannot be
modeled away.

Training compute is approximately `C ≈ 6ND`, so a fixed budget defines a curve in `(N, D)` space
along which the loss can be minimized.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Power law, not exponential — diminishing returns are guaranteed">
  Because loss falls as `N^{-α}` with `α < 1`, each *multiplicative* increase in scale yields a
  *smaller absolute* improvement than the last. The measurement below shows a 10× parameter
  increase buying 0.4204 at first and only 0.0401 four steps later. Progress is real and
  predictable, but it is bought at exponentially rising cost — which is precisely why frontier
  training budgets grew so dramatically for comparatively modest loss gains.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="warning" title="Don't take the tokens-per-parameter number on faith — including from this lesson">
  The famous "≈20 tokens per parameter" figure is a property of the coefficients Hoffmann et al.
  fitted to their own experiments, not a universal constant of nature. The illustrative
  constants used above imply **62.6** instead. Both are computed the same way; they differ
  because the fitted `A`, `B`, `α`, `β` differ.

  What generalizes is the *structure* of the result: for a fixed budget there is a single
  interior optimum, mis-allocating in either direction costs you (about `+0.04` loss at 4× off
  in either direction here), and the penalty curve is fairly flat near the bottom. What does not
  generalize is any specific ratio — that has to be re-fitted for your architecture, data, and
  tokenizer.
</Callout>

## Summary

Loss follows a power law in parameters and data plus an irreducible term `E` representing
language's own entropy — so each 10× in scale buys a smaller absolute gain than the last (0.4204
down to 0.0401 across four steps). For a fixed compute budget `C ≈ 6ND` there is one optimal
split, found here at 5.16B parameters on 323B tokens, with mis-allocation by 4× in either
direction costing about `+0.04` loss. The tokens-per-parameter ratio at that optimum is a
property of the fitted coefficients, not a universal constant: these constants give 62.6, while
Chinchilla's own fit gives roughly 20. The next lesson trains a real GPT end to end.
