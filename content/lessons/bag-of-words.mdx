<h2 id="intuition">The simplest way to turn text into a vector</h2>

Once text is tokenized (last lesson), the next question is: how does a list of words become a
fixed-length numeric vector a model can consume? **Bag-of-words** is the simplest possible
answer: build a **vocabulary** (every unique word across a whole corpus), then represent each
document as a vector counting how many times each vocabulary word appears in it — completely
ignoring word *order*. The name says it directly: a document becomes a "bag" of its words, with
all positional information thrown away.

<h2 id="math">Vocabulary and count vectors</h2>

For a vocabulary of size `V`, document `d`'s bag-of-words vector `x \in \mathbb{R}^V` has:

<MathBlock tex="x_j = \text{count of vocabulary word } j \text{ in document } d" />

Two documents can be compared by treating their count vectors as ordinary vectors — Month 1's
dot product and cosine similarity apply directly, unchanged, to text represented this way.

<Callout kind="tip" title="What bag-of-words throws away, on purpose">
  `"dog bites man"` and `"man bites dog"` produce the *exact same* bag-of-words vector — word
  order is completely discarded. That's a real, deliberate limitation, not an oversight: for
  many tasks (topic classification, spam detection), *which* words appear matters far more than
  their order, so the simplicity is worth the trade. Tasks where order matters a lot (translation,
  next-word prediction) need the sequence-aware models covered starting Month 6.
</Callout>

## Summary

Bag-of-words represents a document as a vector of word counts over a shared vocabulary,
completely discarding word order — a deliberate simplification verified directly above with the
classic "dog bites man" vs. "man bites dog" collision. Despite that limitation, bag-of-words
vectors support real operations like cosine similarity, directly reusing Month 1's vector tools
on text. The next lesson improves on plain counts with TF-IDF, which down-weights words that
appear in nearly every document and are therefore less useful for distinguishing between them.
