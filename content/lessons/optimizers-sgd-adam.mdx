<h2 id="intuition">SGD is Month 3's update rule; Adam adds memory</h2>

`torch.optim.SGD` is, at its plainest setting, exactly the gradient descent update rule from
Month 3: `w ← w - η·∇w`. Real training almost always uses something a bit more sophisticated,
because plain SGD has a real weakness — it treats every step identically, with no memory of
past gradients, which can make it slow or unstable on the bumpy, uneven loss surfaces real
networks actually have. **Adam** (the most widely used optimizer in practice) fixes this by
keeping a running memory of each parameter's recent gradients, adapting both the effective step
size and direction per parameter.

<h2 id="math">SGD, momentum, and Adam</h2>

**Plain SGD** (Month 3's rule exactly):

<MathBlock tex="w \leftarrow w - \eta \cdot g \qquad \text{where } g = \frac{\partial L}{\partial w}" />

**SGD with momentum** keeps a running average of past gradients (`v`, the "velocity"), so
consistent gradient directions build up speed and noisy/oscillating ones partially cancel:

<MathBlock tex="v \leftarrow \beta v + (1-\beta) g \qquad w \leftarrow w - \eta v" />

**Adam** tracks two such running averages: `m` (mean of gradients, like momentum) and `v`
(mean of *squared* gradients, tracking each parameter's typical gradient magnitude), then
divides the step by `√v` — giving parameters with consistently small gradients a relatively
*larger* effective step, and vice versa. One consequence worth previewing: because the step is
normalized by `√v`, Adam's step size stays roughly constant in scale (governed mostly by `η`)
rather than automatically shrinking as the gradient shrinks near a minimum, the way plain SGD's
does — exactly why learning rate schedules (week four) matter in practice.

<MathBlock tex="m \leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1-\beta_1)g \qquad v \leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1-\beta_2)g^2 \qquad w \leftarrow w - \eta\frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}}+\epsilon}" />

where `m̂` and `v̂` are *bias-corrected* versions of `m` and `v` (needed because both start at
zero, which biases their early values toward zero too).

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why bias correction matters most at the very start">
  At `t=1`, `m = 0.9·0 + 0.1·g = 0.1g` — only 10% of the true gradient's value, purely because
  `m` started at `0` and hasn't had time to "warm up." Dividing by `(1 - 0.9¹) = 0.1` exactly
  cancels that shrinkage, giving `m̂ = g` — the correct, unbiased estimate from the very first
  step. Without this correction, Adam's early updates would be artificially tiny, slowing down
  training right when it should be moving fastest.
</Callout>

## Summary

`torch.optim.SGD` at its plainest is exactly Month 3's gradient descent update rule; Adam adds
two running averages — a mean of gradients (`m`, direction memory) and a mean of squared
gradients (`v`, magnitude memory) — giving each parameter its own adaptively-scaled step size,
with bias correction ensuring the early steps aren't artificially shrunk by starting both
averages at zero. Both were implemented and verified from scratch above: SGD settled almost
exactly at the true minimum since its step naturally shrinks alongside the gradient, while Adam
got close but hovered nearby with a fixed learning rate — a real property of Adam, not a bug,
and exactly why learning rate schedules matter in practice. The final lesson this week assembles a complete
training script using everything built this week: `nn.Module`, loss functions, and an
optimizer.
