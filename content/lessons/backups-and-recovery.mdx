<h2 id="intuition">A backup you have never restored is a hypothesis</h2>

Backup strategy collapses into two numbers, and almost every argument about it is really a disagreement about which of them matters more.

<h2 id="math">Two Numbers</h2>

**RPO** — recovery *point* objective: how much data you may lose, set by backup **frequency**.

**RTO** — recovery *time* objective: how long recovery may take, set by restore **speed**.

They are improved by different investments. More frequent backups improve RPO and do nothing for RTO; a hot standby improves RTO and does nothing for RPO. The measured table shows WAL archiving cutting RPO from 1,440 minutes to 5 while *increasing* RTO, because the logs must be replayed.

<Callout kind="danger" title="Recovery time is a claim on your error budget">
  A 40 GB restore at 80 MB/s takes **8.5 minutes** before any replay, DNS change, or cache warm-up. Against a 99.9% SLO's 43-minute monthly budget that bare restore is **20%** of the month's entire allowance — and the WAL-archiving row, at 23.5 minutes, is **54%**, meaning one such event breaks the SLO on its own. Recovery time is not an ops detail; it competes directly with releases for the same budget.
</Callout>

## Summary

RPO is set by backup frequency, RTO by restore speed, and they are improved by different investments — WAL archiving cuts RPO from 1,440 minutes to 5 while raising RTO because logs must replay. Recovery competes with releases for the same error budget: a bare 40 GB restore is 20% of a 99.9% month, and the WAL row at 23.5 minutes is 54%. And a backup that has never been restored is a hypothesis — its failure modes are all invisible until the moment you need it.
