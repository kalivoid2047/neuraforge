<h2 id="intuition">Making a model safer usually makes it slightly worse at things</h2>

Alignment is not free. Each stage that makes a model more helpful, harmless, and honest tends to cost a little raw capability — the **alignment tax**. It is usually worth paying, and pretending it does not exist leads to unpleasant surprises when a benchmark regresses after RLHF.

<h2 id="math">Capability Against Alignment</h2>

The tax appears in the objective directly. RLHF maximizes reward subject to a KL penalty, and DPO inherits the same trade through `β`:

<MathBlock tex="\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}[r(x,y)] - \beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})" />

Pushing harder on preferences moves the policy further from the model that had the capability. That is not a bug in the method — it is the trade the method is *designed* to expose and let you tune.

<Callout kind="tip" title="The exchange rate gets worse, so stop early">
  The measured progression shows this clearly. Early alignment is cheap: SFT bought `+0.59` alignment for `-0.04` capability. Late alignment is expensive: raising `β` from 0.1 to 0.5 bought only `+0.05` alignment for `-0.08` capability — a worse rate in both directions at once. Diminishing returns on one axis and accelerating costs on the other mean the right stopping point is usually earlier than it feels.
</Callout>

## Summary

The alignment tax is the capability cost of alignment training, and it follows directly from the KL-constrained objective: pushing toward preferences moves the policy away from the model that had the capability. Across the stages shown, capability fell `0.78 → 0.63` while alignment rose `0.12 → 0.91`. The exchange rate degrades sharply — SFT bought `+0.59` alignment for `-0.04` capability, while the final beta increase bought `+0.05` for `-0.08`. Comparing rates across stages is how you find the stopping point. The next lesson focuses on the safety axis specifically.
