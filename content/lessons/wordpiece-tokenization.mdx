<h2 id="intuition">Same algorithm shape, a smarter merge criterion</h2>

**WordPiece** (used by BERT and other models) follows the exact same iterative-merge structure
as BPE — start from characters, repeatedly merge, build up a subword vocabulary. The only
change is *which* pair gets merged at each step: instead of picking the single most frequent
adjacent pair, WordPiece picks the pair that most increases the training data's **likelihood**
under a unigram language model — favoring pairs that co-occur *more than their individual
frequencies alone would predict*, not just pairs that are merely common.

<h2 id="math">A different merge score</h2>

WordPiece's merge score for a candidate pair `(A, B)`:

<MathBlock tex="\text{score}(A,B) = \frac{\text{count}(AB)}{\text{count}(A)\times\text{count}(B)}" />

This is closely related to **pointwise mutual information**: a high score means `A` and `B`
appear together far more often than you'd expect if they occurred independently — even if their
*raw* joint count is smaller than some other pair's. A pair like `("t", "h")` might have a huge
raw count simply because both `"t"` and `"h"` are individually extremely common letters, even
if they don't have any special affinity for each other; WordPiece's score divides that raw count
down, correctly reflecting that.

<Callout kind="tip" title="A concrete divergence, verified above">
  On this corpus, BPE merges `('e','s')` — the single highest raw count (9), driven by `e` and
  `s` both appearing across *several* different words. WordPiece instead merges `('i','d')` —
  a pair with a much lower raw count, but `i` and `d` are each individually **rare** (both occur
  *only* in `"widest"`), so dividing by their small individual counts produces the highest score
  of any candidate pair. WordPiece is explicitly rewarding a pair that co-occurs unusually
  reliably relative to how rare its two symbols are elsewhere, even though it appears less often
  in absolute terms.
</Callout>

## Summary

WordPiece uses the same iterative merge-building structure as BPE, but scores candidate pairs
by `count(AB)/(count(A)×count(B))` rather than raw frequency — a mutual-information-like measure
favoring genuine co-occurrence affinity over pairs that are merely frequent because their
individual symbols are common, verified directly above by comparing both scoring methods on the
same corpus. The next lesson covers SentencePiece, which changes the framing once more — treating
tokenization itself as a probabilistic model to optimize, rather than a sequence of greedy merges.
