<h2 id="intuition">A weighted vote, then a yes/no decision</h2>

A perceptron takes several numeric inputs, each multiplied by its own learned weight, sums them
up, and outputs one of two classes based on whether that sum clears a threshold. It's a linear
classifier — literally the dot product from Month 1, plus a decision rule.

<h2 id="math">The formula, precisely</h2>

<MathBlock tex="z = \vec{w}\cdot\vec{x} + b, \qquad \hat{y} = \begin{cases}1 & \text{if } z \ge 0\\ 0 & \text{if } z < 0\end{cases}" />

`w` is the weight vector (one weight per input feature), `x` is the input vector, and `b` is the
**bias** — a single extra learned number, independent of any input, that shifts the decision
threshold.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why the bias term is necessary, not decorative">
  Without `b`, the decision boundary `w·x = 0` is forced to pass exactly through the origin —
  every perceptron without a bias can only separate classes along a line (or plane) through
  `(0, 0, ...)`. Real data is essentially never conveniently centered that way. The bias is what
  lets the decision boundary sit anywhere, not just through the origin — genuinely necessary
  representational capacity, not a minor convenience.
</Callout>

<h2 id="python">Implementing the forward computation</h2>

<Callout kind="tip" title="These weights were hand-designed, not learned -- yet">
  Finding `bias = -1.5` for AND and `bias = -0.5` for OR by hand is exactly the kind of thing
  you don't want to do for real problems with dozens or thousands of inputs. The next lesson
  builds the **learning algorithm** that finds good weights and bias automatically from labeled
  examples, instead of you guessing them.
</Callout>

## Summary

A perceptron computes `z = w·x + b`, then outputs 1 if `z ≥ 0` and 0 otherwise — a dot product
(Month 1) plus a threshold decision. The bias `b` is what lets the decision boundary sit
anywhere in space, not just through the origin, which is genuinely necessary for real,
off-center data. The weights and bias used above were hand-designed for illustration; the next
lesson builds an algorithm that learns them from labeled data instead.
