<h2 id="intuition">Zero probability is catastrophic, not merely inaccurate</h2>

Last lesson's bigram model assigned `-inf` log-probability to "the mat ate the cat" — because
one single bigram in it was never observed in training. That's not a small inaccuracy: because
the chain rule *multiplies* per-token probabilities, **a single unseen n-gram forces the entire
sequence's probability to exactly zero**, regardless of how ordinary every other part of the
sentence was. **Smoothing** fixes this by reserving some probability mass for unseen events —
exactly the same problem, and the same `+1` solution, as Month 5's Naive Bayes classifier.

<h2 id="math">Smoothing strategies</h2>

**Add-k (Laplace) smoothing** adds a small constant to every count, including unseen ones:

<MathBlock tex="P_{\text{add-}k}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \frac{\text{count}(w_{i-1}, w_i) + k}{\text{count}(w_{i-1}) + k|V|}" />

**Backoff** takes a different approach: if the bigram was never seen, *fall back* to the unigram
probability of `w_i` alone (which is much more likely to have been observed), scaled by a
discount factor:

<MathBlock tex="P_{\text{backoff}}(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) = \begin{cases} P(w_i \mid w_{i-1}) & \text{if count}(w_{i-1},w_i) > 0 \\ \lambda \cdot P(w_i) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}" />

<Callout kind="tip" title="The sparsity trade-off behind choosing n">
  A larger `n` captures more context (a 5-gram model knows far more about what precedes a word
  than a bigram does) — but n-gram counts get *exponentially* sparser as `n` grows, since the
  number of possible n-grams grows as `|V|^n` while the corpus size stays fixed. Past a point,
  almost every n-gram in new text was never seen in training, and the model is smoothing or
  backing off constantly, learning little. This inherent tension — more context vs. unusable
  sparsity — is precisely what neural language models (next lesson) sidestep, by learning dense
  representations that generalize across contexts instead of counting exact sequences.
</Callout>

## Summary

Because the chain rule multiplies per-token probabilities, a single unseen n-gram zeroes out an
entire sequence's probability — a catastrophic failure, not a small inaccuracy. Add-k smoothing
distributes a little probability mass to every possible continuation; backoff falls back to the
lower-order (unigram) estimate when a bigram is unseen; both convert `-inf` into a low but
finite score, verified directly above. Larger `n` captures more context but makes counts
exponentially sparser — a tension neural models resolve differently. The next lesson covers
perplexity, the standard metric for comparing language models like these.
