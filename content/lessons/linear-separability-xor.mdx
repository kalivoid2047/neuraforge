<h2 id="intuition">The single most important limitation of a single perceptron</h2>

Last lesson's convergence guarantee had a precondition: the data has to be **linearly
separable** — a single straight line (in 2D; a hyperplane in higher dimensions) has to exist
that perfectly divides the two classes. AND and OR both satisfy this. **XOR does not**, and
proving that concretely is one of the most historically consequential examples in the field —
it's a large part of why research interest in neural networks stalled for years after Minsky
and Papert's 1969 book pointed it out.

<Callout kind="tip" title="Linear separability, defined">
  A dataset with two classes is linearly separable if there exists some line (2D) or hyperplane
  (higher dimensions) that has every point of one class strictly on one side, and every point of
  the other class strictly on the other side.
</Callout>

<h2 id="math">XOR's truth table, and why no line works</h2>

<MathBlock tex="\text{XOR}(0,0)=0 \quad \text{XOR}(0,1)=1 \quad \text{XOR}(1,0)=1 \quad \text{XOR}(1,1)=0" />

Plot these four points on a 2D grid: `(0,0)` and `(1,1)` are class 0; `(0,1)` and `(1,0)` are
class 1. The class-0 points sit on one diagonal, the class-1 points on the *other* diagonal —
try to draw any single straight line separating them, and you'll find the two diagonals are
interleaved in a way no single line can untangle.

<Callout kind="danger" title="Why this mattered historically">
  Minsky & Papert's 1969 book *Perceptrons* proved this limitation rigorously and generally (not
  just for XOR specifically), and its reception is widely credited with contributing to reduced
  funding and interest in neural network research through much of the 1970s — sometimes called
  the first "AI winter." The resolution wasn't found until the popularization of
  **multi-layer** networks with a working training algorithm (backpropagation, Month 3) —
  stacking perceptron-like units in layers turns out to escape this limitation entirely, which
  is exactly where week four of this month is headed.
</Callout>

## Summary

Linear separability requires a single line or hyperplane to perfectly divide two classes; AND
and OR satisfy this, but XOR provably does not — its two classes sit on interleaved diagonals
that no single line can untangle. This is a genuine, provable limitation of a single perceptron,
historically significant enough to have contributed to reduced neural network research funding
in the 1970s. The fix — stacking multiple layers — is where this month's remaining weeks head.

## Further reading

- Minsky & Papert, *Perceptrons* (1969) — the book that proved this limitation rigorously and
  shaped a decade of the field's research direction.
