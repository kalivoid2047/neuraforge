<h2 id="intuition">Reversing the direction of "given"</h2>

Often you know `P(evidence | hypothesis)` — e.g., "if a document is spam, how likely is the word
'free' to appear?" — but what you actually want is `P(hypothesis | evidence)`: "given the word
'free' appeared, how likely is this document spam?" **Bayes' theorem** is the tool that flips
conditional probability around, and it's the mathematical foundation of the Naive Bayes text
classifier built later this month.

<h2 id="math">Reversing the condition</h2>

Starting from last lesson's definition, `P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B)`, and noting
`P(A∩B) = P(B|A)P(A)` (the same joint probability, written the other way):

<MathBlock tex="P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}" />

In the classic naming: `P(A)` is the **prior** (belief before seeing evidence), `P(B|A)` is the
**likelihood** (how probable the evidence is, if the hypothesis is true), and `P(A|B)` is the
**posterior** (updated belief after seeing evidence).

<Callout kind="tip" title="Why the prior matters so much: the base-rate trap">
  A medical test that's "99% accurate" sounds like it should make a positive result very
  trustworthy. But if the disease itself is very rare (a low prior), most positive results can
  still be false positives — because there are so many more healthy people than sick people that
  even a small false-positive *rate* produces more false positives in absolute count than true
  positives from the (rare) sick population. The code below computes this exactly.
</Callout>

<h2 id="python">Bayes' theorem, computed exactly and verified by simulation</h2>

<CodeCell
  code={"import numpy as np\n\n# a disease affecting 1% of the population; test is 99% accurate both ways\n# (99% chance of positive if sick, 99% chance of negative if healthy)\np_disease = 0.01\np_positive_given_disease = 0.99       # sensitivity\np_negative_given_healthy = 0.99       # specificity\np_positive_given_healthy = 1 - p_negative_given_healthy   # false positive rate\n\n# P(positive) via the law of total probability: sum over both ways to get a positive result\np_positive = (p_positive_given_disease * p_disease) + (p_positive_given_healthy * (1 - p_disease))\n\n# Bayes' theorem: P(disease | positive)\np_disease_given_positive = (p_positive_given_disease * p_disease) / p_positive\n\nprint(\"exact calculation via Bayes' theorem:\")\nprint(f\"  P(disease) = {p_disease}  (the prior -- disease is rare)\")\nprint(f\"  P(positive | disease) = {p_positive_given_disease}  (the likelihood -- test sensitivity)\")\nprint(f\"  P(disease | positive) = {p_disease_given_positive:.4f}  (the posterior -- what we actually want)\")\nprint()\nprint(f\"despite a '99% accurate' test, a positive result means only a\")\nprint(f\"{p_disease_given_positive*100:.1f}% chance of actually having the disease!\")\nprint()\n\n# verify by simulating a large population directly\nrng = np.random.default_rng(0)\nn = 5_000_000\nhas_disease = rng.random(n) < p_disease\ntest_positive = np.where(\n    has_disease,\n    rng.random(n) < p_positive_given_disease,     # sick people: positive with 99% prob\n    rng.random(n) < p_positive_given_healthy,     # healthy people: positive with 1% prob (false positive)\n)\n\nsimulated_posterior = has_disease[test_positive].mean()\nprint(f\"simulated P(disease | positive) over {n:,} people: {simulated_posterior:.4f}\")\nprint(f\"matches exact calculation: {np.isclose(simulated_posterior, p_disease_given_positive, atol=0.005)}\")"}
  output={"exact calculation via Bayes' theorem:\n  P(disease) = 0.01  (the prior -- disease is rare)\n  P(positive | disease) = 0.99  (the likelihood -- test sensitivity)\n  P(disease | positive) = 0.5000  (the posterior -- what we actually want)\n\ndespite a '99% accurate' test, a positive result means only a\n50.0% chance of actually having the disease!\n\nsimulated P(disease | positive) over 5,000,000 people: 0.4991\nmatches exact calculation: True"}

## Summary

Bayes' theorem, `P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B)`, reverses the direction of conditioning, converting a
known likelihood into the posterior probability actually needed. The base-rate example — a
seemingly accurate test producing a surprisingly low posterior for a rare condition — shows
directly why the prior can't be ignored, verified both by exact calculation and by simulating
millions of individuals. The next lesson formalizes random variables and their distributions,
the objects Bayes' theorem and everything else this month actually operates on.
